# Alice's Adventures in Wonderland (2026 Modern Edition)

*By Lewis Carroll — Modernized into Clear, Everyday 2026 English for All Ages*

---

# Chapter 1: Down the Rabbit-Hole

Alice was beginning to get thoroughly tired of sitting next to her sister on the grassy bank with nothing to do. Once or twice she had peeked into the book her sister was reading, but it didn't have any pictures or conversations in it. "And what is the point of a book," Alice thought, "without pictures or conversations?"

So she was turning over in her mind—as well as she could, since the hot afternoon made her feel awfully sleepy and sluggish—whether the pleasure of weaving a daisy chain would be worth the trouble of getting up and picking the daisies, when suddenly a White Rabbit with pink eyes dashed right past her.

There wasn't anything so *very* remarkable about that, nor did Alice think it so *very* strange to hear the Rabbit mutter to itself, "Oh dear! Oh dear! I'm going to be late!" (When she thought it over later, it occurred to her that she really ought to have wondered at this, but at the time it all seemed completely natural.) But when the Rabbit actually *pulled a pocket watch out of its vest pocket*, checked the time, and hurried on, Alice leaped to her feet. It flashed across her mind that she had never before seen a rabbit with a vest pocket, let alone a watch to take out of it! Burning with curiosity, she ran across the field after it, arriving just in time to see it pop down a large rabbit hole under the hedge.

A second later, down went Alice right after it, never once stopping to consider how on earth she was going to get out again.

The rabbit hole ran straight on like a tunnel for a little way, and then plunged suddenly downward—so suddenly that Alice didn't have a single second to think about stopping herself before she found herself falling down an extraordinarily deep well.

Either the well was unbelievably deep, or she was falling very slowly, because she had plenty of time on the way down to look around and wonder what was going to happen next. First, she tried to peer down and make out what was coming, but it was far too dark to see anything. Then she looked at the walls of the shaft and noticed they were lined with cupboards and bookshelves. Here and there, she saw maps and pictures hanging from pegs. As she floated past one of the shelves, she grabbed a jar. It was labeled **ORANGE MARMALADE**, but to her great disappointment, it was completely empty. She didn't want to drop the jar for fear of crushing someone below, so she managed to tuck it into one of the cupboards as she drifted past.

"Well!" Alice thought to herself. "After a fall like this, tumbling down the stairs will be a piece of cake! Everyone at home will think I'm so brave! Why, I wouldn't say a word about it even if I fell off the roof of the house!" (Which was almost certainly true.)

Down, down, down. Was the fall *never* going to come to an end? "I wonder how many miles I've fallen by now?" she said out loud. "I must be getting somewhere near the center of the Earth. Let me see: that would be about four thousand miles down, I think—" (Alice had learned a number of things like this during lessons in the schoolroom, and although this wasn't exactly a great opportunity to show off her knowledge since there was no one around to listen, it was still good practice to recite it) "—yes, that's about the right distance. But then, I wonder what Latitude or Longitude I've reached?" (Alice didn't have the foggiest idea what Latitude was, or Longitude either, but she thought they were wonderfully grand words to say.)

Before long, she started up again. "I wonder if I'll fall right *through* the Earth! How funny it'll be to pop out among the people who walk with their heads hanging down! The Antipathies, I think—" (she was rather glad nobody was listening this time, since that didn't sound quite like the right word) "—though I'll have to ask them what the country is called, you know. 'Excuse me, ma'am, is this New Zealand or Australia?'" (And she tried to curtsy as she spoke—just imagine curtsying while tumbling through the air! Do you think you could manage it?) "'And what an ignorant little girl she'll think I am for asking! No, it will never do to ask. Maybe I'll see the name written up somewhere.'"

Down, down, down. There was nothing else to do, so Alice soon started talking again. "Dinah will miss me terribly tonight, I'm sure!" (Dinah was the cat.) "I hope they remember her saucer of milk at tea time. Oh, Dinah dear! I wish you were down here with me! There aren't any mice in the air, I'm afraid, but you might catch a bat, and a bat is very much like a mouse, you know. But do cats eat bats, I wonder?" Here Alice began to feel rather sleepy, and she went on repeating to herself in a dreamy, hypnotic voice, "Do cats eat bats? Do cats eat bats?" and sometimes, "Do bats eat cats?" Because, you see, since she couldn't answer either question, it didn't really matter which way she put it. She felt herself nodding off and had just started dreaming that she was walking hand in hand with Dinah, asking her very seriously, "Now, Dinah, tell me the honest truth: have you ever eaten a bat?" when suddenly—*thump! thump!*—down she landed on a heap of dry sticks and leaves, and the fall was over.

Alice wasn't hurt in the slightest, and she leaped to her feet in an instant. She looked up, but overhead was pitch black. Before her stretched another long corridor, and the White Rabbit was still in sight, hurrying down it. There wasn't a second to lose! Off sprinted Alice like the wind, catching up just in time to hear him squeak as he rounded a corner: "Oh, my ears and whiskers, look how late it's getting!" She was right on his heels when she turned the corner, but the Rabbit had completely vanished. Instead, she found herself in a long, low-ceilinged hall illuminated by a row of lamps hanging from the roof.

There were doors all around the hall, but every single one of them was locked. After Alice had walked all the way down one side and back up the other, trying every doorknob, she walked dejectedly down the middle, wondering how she was ever going to get out again.

Suddenly, she came upon a little three-legged table made entirely of solid glass. There was nothing on it except a tiny golden key, and Alice's first thought was that it might belong to one of the doors in the hall. But alas! Either the locks were too large or the key was too small, but in any case, it wouldn't open a single one. Walking around the hall a second time, however, she noticed a low curtain she hadn't spotted before, and behind it was a little door about fifteen inches high. She tried the tiny golden key in the lock, and to her immense delight, it fitted!

Alice opened the door and found that it led into a small passage, hardly bigger than a rat hole. She knelt down and peered along the tunnel into the loveliest garden you ever saw. How she ached to get out of that gloomy hall and wander among those beds of bright flowers and cool, splashing fountains! But she couldn't even squeeze her head through the doorway. "And even if my head would fit," poor Alice thought, "it wouldn't be much use without my shoulders. Oh, how I wish I could collapse like a telescope! I think I could, if only I knew where to begin." You see, so many extraordinary things had happened lately that Alice had begun to think very few things were truly impossible.

There seemed to be no point in waiting by the little door, so she went back to the table, half hoping she might find another key, or at least an instruction book on how to fold people up like telescopes. This time, she found a little bottle on it ("Which definitely wasn't here before," Alice noted). Tied around the neck of the bottle was a paper tag, with the words **DRINK ME** beautifully printed in large letters.

It was all very well to say "Drink me," but sensible little Alice wasn't going to do *that* in a hurry. "No, I'll check first," she said, "and see whether it's marked *poison* or not." She had read several cautionary tales about children who had been burned, or eaten alive by wild beasts and other unpleasant things, all because they *refused* to remember the simple rules their friends had taught them—such as, a red-hot fire poker will burn you if you hold it too long; if you cut your finger *deeply* with a knife, it usually bleeds; and she had never forgotten that if you drink much from a bottle marked "Poison," it is almost certain to make you sick sooner or later.

However, this bottle was *not* marked "Poison," so Alice decided to take a cautious sip. Finding it absolutely delicious (in fact, it tasted like a blend of cherry tart, creamy custard, pineapple, roast turkey, buttery toffee, and hot buttered toast), she quickly finished it off.

***

"What a strange sensation!" Alice exclaimed. "I must be collapsing like a telescope!"

And so she was, indeed! She was now only ten inches tall, and her face lit up at the thought that she was finally the right size to go through the little door into that lovely garden. First, though, she waited a few minutes to see if she was going to shrink any further. She felt a bit nervous about this. "Because it might end up," Alice said to herself, "with me going out completely, like a candle! I wonder what I'd be like then?" And she tried to imagine what the flame of a candle looks like after it's blown out, though she couldn't remember ever seeing such a thing.

After a while, finding that nothing more was happening, she decided to head for the garden at once. But oh, poor Alice! When she got to the door, she realized she had forgotten the little golden key. When she went back to the table for it, she found that she couldn't possibly reach it! She could see it quite clearly through the glass, and she tried her hardest to scramble up one of the table legs, but it was far too slippery. When she had completely exhausted herself with trying, the poor little thing sat down on the floor and cried.

"Come on now, there's no use in crying like that!" Alice told herself rather sharply. "I advise you to stop this instant!" She generally gave herself very good advice (even if she rarely followed it), and sometimes she scolded herself so sternly that she brought tears to her own eyes. Once, she remembered trying to box her own ears for cheating in a game of croquet she was playing against herself, because this quirky child was extraordinarily fond of pretending to be two people. "Though it's completely useless now," poor Alice thought, "to pretend to be two people! Why, there's barely enough of me left to make *one* respectable person!"

Soon, her eyes fell on a little glass box lying under the table. She opened it and found a very small cake inside, on which the words **EAT ME** were beautifully spelled out in currants. "Well, I'll eat it," said Alice, "and if it makes me grow bigger, I can reach the key. If it makes me grow smaller, I can creep under the door. So either way, I'll get into the garden, and I don't care which happens!"

She took a small bite and said anxiously to herself, "Which way? Which way?" holding her hand on top of her head to feel which direction she was growing. She was quite surprised to find that she stayed exactly the same size. Of course, that's what usually happens when someone eats cake, but Alice had gotten so used to expecting nothing but extraordinary things to happen that it seemed terribly dull and foolish for life to go on in the ordinary way.

So she set to work, and very quickly polished off the rest of the cake.

# Chapter 2: The Pool of Tears

"Curiouser and curiouser!" cried Alice (she was so shocked that for the moment she completely forgot how to speak proper grammar). "Now I'm stretching out like the biggest telescope that ever existed! Goodbye, feet!" (Because when she looked down at her feet, they were so far off they were practically out of sight.) "Oh, my poor little feet, I wonder who will put on your socks and shoes for you now, darlings? I'm sure *I* won't be able to! I'll be way too far away to bother with you. You'll just have to manage as best you can—though I'd better be kind to them," Alice thought, "or maybe they won't walk where I want to go! Let's see: I'll send them a new pair of boots every Christmas."

And she went on planning to herself how she was going to manage it. "They'll have to go by delivery courier," she thought. "How funny it will seem, sending presents to your own feet! And how bizarre the address label will look:

> *Alice's Right Foot, Esq.*  
> *The Hearthrug (near the Fireplace Fender)*  
> *(With Alice's love)*

Oh dear, what utter nonsense I'm talking!"

Just then, her head bumped against the ceiling of the hall. In fact, she was now over nine feet tall! She immediately snatched up the little golden key and hurried over to the garden door.

Poor Alice! All she could manage, lying flat on her side, was to peek through into the garden with a single eye. But squeezing through was more hopeless than ever. She sat down and began to weep all over again.

"You ought to be ashamed of yourself," Alice scolded, "a great big girl like you" (and she certainly had a point!) "crying like this! Stop it this instant, I tell you!" But she went right on crying anyway, shedding gallons of tears until a large pool formed all around her, about four inches deep and stretching halfway down the hall.

After a while, she heard the soft pitter-patter of little feet in the distance, and she hastily wiped her eyes to see what was coming. It was the White Rabbit returning, splendidly dressed, carrying a pair of fancy white kidskin gloves in one hand and a large folding fan in the other. He came trotting along in a tremendous rush, muttering to himself as he came, "Oh! The Duchess, the Duchess! Won't she be furious if I've kept her waiting!" Alice felt so desperate that she was ready to ask anyone for help, so when the Rabbit drew near, she began in a small, timid voice, "Excuse me, sir—" The Rabbit leaped out of his skin, dropped the white gloves and the fan, and scurried off into the darkness as fast as his legs could carry him.

Alice picked up the fan and the gloves, and since the hall was terribly warm, she kept fanning herself as she continued talking to herself: "Good grief! How strange everything is today! And yesterday everything went along just as usual. I wonder if I was transformed during the night? Let me think: was I the same when I got up this morning? I almost feel like I remember feeling a little different. But if I'm not the same, the next question is: who in the world am I? Ah, *that's* the great mystery!" And she began going over all the kids she knew who were her age, to see if she could have been swapped for any of them.

"I'm certain I'm not Ada," she said, "because her hair falls in such long ringlets, and mine doesn't curl at all. And I definitely can't be Mabel, because I know all sorts of things, and she—oh, she knows so very little! Besides, *she's* she, and *I'm* I, and—oh dear, what a puzzle it all is! Let me check if I still know all the things I used to know. Let's see: four times five is twelve, four times six is thirteen, and four times seven is—oh no, I'll never get to twenty at this rate! But never mind the multiplication tables, let's try Geography. London is the capital of Paris, and Paris is the capital of Rome, and Rome—no, *that's* completely wrong, I'm certain! I really must have been turned into Mabel! Let me try reciting 'How doth the little—'" She folded her hands primly on her lap as if she were in the classroom, and began to repeat it, but her voice sounded scratchy and weird, and the words didn't come out the way they used to:

> "How does the little crocodile  
> Improve his shiny tail,  
> And pour the waters of the Nile  
> On every golden scale!  
>  
> How cheerfully he seems to grin,  
> How neatly spreads his claws,  
> And welcomes little fishes in  
> With gently smiling jaws!"  

"I'm positive those aren't the right words," poor Alice said, her eyes filling with tears again as she went on. "I must be Mabel after all, and I'll have to go live in that cramped little house, with practically no toys to play with, and oh, endless lessons to memorize! No, I've made up my mind: if I'm Mabel, I'll stay down here! It won't do them any good to poke their heads down and call, 'Come back up, sweetie!' I'll just look up and say, 'Who am I, then? Tell me that first, and then, if I like being that person, I'll come up. If not, I'll stay down here until I'm somebody else!' But oh..." cried Alice with a sudden burst of tears, "I really wish they *would* poke their heads down! I am so, so tired of being all alone here!"

As she said this, she looked down at her hands and was startled to see that she had slipped on one of the Rabbit's little white kidskin gloves while talking. "How on earth did I do *that*?" she wondered. "I must be shrinking again!" She got up and hurried to the table to measure herself against it, finding that, by her best estimate, she was now only about two feet tall and shrinking fast. She soon realized that the cause was the fan she was holding, and she dropped it instantly—just in the nick of time to avoid shrinking away into nothingness.

"That *was* a close call!" Alice said, thoroughly frightened by the sudden change, but immensely relieved to find herself still in existence. "And now for the garden!" She dashed as fast as she could back to the little door. But alas! The little door was locked tight again, and the tiny golden key was lying on top of the glass table just like before. "And things are worse than ever," the poor child thought, "because I've never been this small before, never! And honestly, it's totally unfair, that's what it is!"

Just as she said these words, her foot slipped, and the next second—*splash!*—she was up to her chin in salty water. Her first thought was that she had somehow fallen into the sea. "And in that case, I can take the train home," she told herself. (Alice had been to the beach once in her life, and had come to the general conclusion that wherever you go along the English coast, you find rows of changing huts out in the water, children digging in the sand with wooden shovels, a line of boarding houses, and a train station right behind them.) Before long, however, she realized the truth: she was swimming in the massive pool of tears she had wept when she was nine feet tall.

"I wish I hadn't cried so much!" Alice said as she paddled around, trying to find her way out. "Now I suppose I'll be punished by drowning in my own tears! That *will* be a bizarre fate, for sure! But then again, absolutely everything is bizarre today."

Just then, she heard something splashing in the pool a little way off, and she swam closer to make out what it was. At first, she thought it must be a walrus or a hippopotamus, but then she remembered how tiny she was now, and she soon realized it was only a mouse that had slipped in just like her.

"Would there be any use," Alice thought, "in trying to talk to this mouse? Everything is so upside down down here that it seems very likely it can talk. At any rate, there's no harm in trying." So she began: "O Mouse, do you know the way out of this pool? I am very tired of swimming around here, O Mouse!" (Alice assumed this must be the proper way to speak to a mouse. She had never spoken to one before, but she remembered seeing the declension in her brother's Latin grammar book: "A mouse—of a mouse—to a mouse—a mouse—O mouse!") The Mouse looked at her rather curiously and seemed to give a tiny wink with one of its little eyes, but it said nothing.

"Maybe it doesn't speak English," Alice thought. "I bet it's a French mouse that came over with William the Conqueror." (For all her knowledge of history, Alice had no clear notion of how long ago anything had actually happened.) So she tried again: "*Où est ma chatte?*" ("Where is my cat?"), which was the first sentence in her French textbook. The Mouse leaped right out of the water in a panic, trembling all over with fright. "Oh, I beg your pardon!" Alice cried hastily, terrified that she had hurt the poor creature's feelings. "I completely forgot you don't like cats!"

"*Not like cats!*" cried the Mouse in a shrill, furious voice. "Would *you* like cats if you were in my place?"

"Well, maybe not," Alice said soothingly. "Please don't be angry about it. And yet, I really wish I could show you our cat Dinah. I think you'd take a liking to cats if you could only see her. She is such a sweet, quiet little thing," Alice continued dreamily as she paddled lazily through the pool. "She curls up purring so nicely by the fire, licking her paws and washing her face, and she's so delightfully soft to cuddle—plus she's an absolute champion at catching mice—oh, I beg your pardon!" Alice cried again, for this time the Mouse was bristling all over, and she felt certain he was deeply offended. "We won't talk about her anymore if you'd rather not."

"*We*, indeed!" squeaked the Mouse, who was trembling right down to the tip of his tail. "As if *I* would ever speak on such a subject! Our family has always *hated* cats—nasty, cruel, vulgar creatures! Don't let me hear that name again!"

"I won't! Truly!" Alice said, in a frantic hurry to change the subject. "Are you... are you fond of... dogs?" The Mouse did not answer, so Alice went on eagerly: "There's such a lovely little dog near our house that I'd love to show you! A spunky little bright-eyed terrier, you know, with oh, such long curly brown hair! He fetches things when you throw them, sits up and begs for his dinner, and does all kinds of tricks—I can't even remember half of them! He belongs to a farmer nearby, who says he's so useful he's worth a fortune! He says the dog kills all the rats and—oh dear!" Alice cried sorrowfully. "I'm afraid I've offended him again!" For the Mouse was paddling away from her as fast as his little legs could go, churning up quite a commotion in the pool as he went.

So she called softly after him, "Dear little Mouse! Please come back, and we won't talk about cats or dogs at all, if you don't like them!" When the Mouse heard this, he turned around and swam slowly back to her. His face was quite pale (from intense emotion, Alice thought), and he said in a low, trembling voice, "Let us get to the shore, and then I will tell you my life story, and you'll understand why it is I hate cats and dogs so much."

It was high time to get out, for the pool was getting quite crowded with all the birds and animals that had fallen into it. There were a Duck and a Dodo, a Lory parrot and an Eaglet, along with several other eccentric creatures. With Alice leading the way, the whole party swam to the shore.

# Chapter 3: A Caucus-Race and a Long Tale

It was quite a bizarre-looking crowd that gathered on the riverbank: birds with soaked, drooping feathers, animals with fur plastered flat against their bodies, and everyone dripping wet, cranky, and thoroughly miserable.

The first question, of course, was how to get dry again. They held a conference to discuss it, and after a few minutes, it felt completely natural to Alice to find herself chatting casually with them, as if they had known each other her whole life. In fact, she got into quite a long argument with the Lory, who eventually turned sulky and would only insist, "I am older than you, so I must know better!" But Alice refused to concede that without knowing how old the bird actually was. Since the Lory flatly refused to give its age, the debate came to a complete standstill.

Finally, the Mouse—who seemed to be regarded as a figure of authority among them—called out, "Sit down, all of you, and listen to me! *I’ll* soon make you dry enough!"

Everyone sat down immediately in a wide circle with the Mouse right in the center. Alice kept her eyes nervously glued to it, convinced she would catch a terrible cold if she didn’t dry off very soon.

"Ahem!" the Mouse began with an important air. "Is everyone ready? This is the driest thing I know. Complete silence all around, if you please! 'William the Conqueror, whose cause was favored by the Pope, soon secured the submission of the English, who lacked leaders and had lately grown quite accustomed to usurpation and conquest. Edwin and Morcar, the Earls of Mercia and Northumbria—'"

"Ugh!" groaned the Lory with a shudder.

"I beg your pardon?" the Mouse said, frowning, but maintaining a very polite tone. "Did you speak?"

"Not me!" the Lory replied hastily.

"I thought you did," said the Mouse. "—I proceed. 'Edwin and Morcar, the Earls of Mercia and Northumbria, declared their support for him; and even Stigand, the patriotic Archbishop of Canterbury, found it advisable—'"

"Found *what*?" asked the Duck.

"Found *it*," the Mouse snapped, rather annoyed. "Surely you know what 'it' means!"

"I know what 'it' means well enough when *I* find something," said the Duck. "Usually it’s a frog or a worm. The question is, what did the archbishop find?"

The Mouse ignored the question entirely and pressed on hurriedly: "'—found it advisable to accompany Edgar the Atheling to meet William and offer him the crown. William’s conduct was moderate at first, but the insolence of his Normans—' How are you coming along now, my dear?" it asked, turning toward Alice.

"Just as wet as ever," Alice replied mournfully. "It doesn’t seem to be drying me at all."

"In that case," the Dodo declared solemnly, rising to its feet, "I move that the meeting adjourn for the immediate adoption of more energetic remedies—"

"Speak English!" cut in the Eaglet. "I don’t know what half those big words mean, and what’s more, I don’t believe you do either!" The Eaglet ducked its head to hide a smirk, and several other birds giggled out loud.

"What I was *trying* to say," the Dodo said with offended dignity, "is that the best thing to dry us off would be a Caucus-race."

"What *is* a Caucus-race?" Alice asked. Not that she particularly cared to know, but the Dodo had paused as though expecting *someone* to speak up, and nobody else seemed inclined to say a thing.

"Why," said the Dodo, "the best way to explain it is to just do it." (And in case you’d like to try it yourself on a cold winter day, I’ll tell you how the Dodo ran it.)

First, it marked out a track in a rough circle ("The exact shape doesn't really matter," it explained). Then the whole crowd was placed along the course, scattered here and there. There was no "Ready, set, go!" Everyone simply started running whenever they felt like it and stopped whenever they wanted, so it wasn’t at all easy to know when the race was over.

Still, once they had been running around for about half an hour and were completely dry again, the Dodo suddenly called out, "The race is over!"

They all swarmed around it, panting for breath and asking, "So who won?"

That was a question the Dodo couldn’t answer without some serious thought. It sat for a long time with one finger pressed against its forehead (the classic thinking pose Shakespeare always strikes in portraits), while everyone else waited in silence.

At last, the Dodo announced, "*Everyone* has won, and all must have prizes!"

"But who is giving out the prizes?" quite a chorus of voices asked.

"Why, *she* is, of course," said the Dodo, pointing a finger straight at Alice.

Instantly the entire crowd swarmed around her, shouting in a confused scramble, "Prizes! Prizes!"

Alice had no idea what to do. In desperation, she reached into her pocket and pulled out a box of candies (luckily, the salty water hadn’t gotten inside) and passed them out as prizes. There was exactly one for everyone, all the way around.

"Wait, she has to have a prize herself, you know," said the Mouse.

"Of course," the Dodo replied with great gravity. "What else do you have in your pocket?" it asked, turning to Alice.

"Only a thimble," Alice said sadly.

"Hand it over here," said the Dodo.

Then they all gathered around her once more while the Dodo solemnly presented the thimble, saying, "We beg your acceptance of this elegant thimble." When it finished this short speech, the whole crowd cheered enthusiastically.

Alice thought the entire ceremony was utterly ridiculous, but everyone looked so dead serious that she didn’t dare laugh. Since she couldn’t think of anything to say, she simply bowed, accepted the thimble, and tried to look as solemn as possible.

The next order of business was eating the candies. This created quite a commotion, as the larger birds complained that they couldn’t even taste theirs, while the smaller ones choked and had to be patted on the back. Eventually, the chaos died down, and they all sat in a circle again, begging the Mouse to tell them another story.

"You promised to tell me your history, remember?" Alice said. "And why it is you hate—C and D," she added in a whisper, half-afraid it would take offense again.

"Mine is a long and a sad tale!" the Mouse said, turning to Alice with a heavy sigh.

"It *is* a long tail, certainly," Alice remarked, looking down in wonder at the Mouse’s actual tail. "But why do you call it sad?"

And she kept puzzling over the question while the Mouse was speaking, so in her imagination, her picture of the tale looked something like this:

> “Fury said to a  
>   mouse, that he  
>     met in the house,  
>       ‘Let us both go  
>         to court: *I* will  
>           prosecute *you*!  
>             Come, I’ll take  
>               no refusal; we  
>                 must have a trial,  
>                   for really this  
>                     morning I’ve  
>                       nothing to do.’  
>                         Said the mouse  
>                           to the cur,  
>                             ‘Such a trial,  
>                               dear sir, with  
>                                 no judge or jury,  
>                                   would just waste  
>                                     all our breath.’  
>                                       ‘I’ll be judge,  
>                                         I’ll be jury,’  
>                                           said cunning  
>                                             old Fury:  
>                                               ‘I’ll try  
>                                                 the whole case,  
>                                                   and condemn  
>                                                     you to  
>                                                       death!’”  

"You’re not paying attention!" the Mouse scolded Alice severely. "What on earth are you thinking about?"

"I beg your pardon," Alice said very meekly. "You had gotten to the fifth bend, I think?"

"I had *not!*" cried the Mouse, sharp and furious.

"A knot!" said Alice, always eager to make herself useful, looking around anxiously. "Oh, please let me help untie it!"

"I shall do nothing of the kind!" the Mouse snapped, getting to its feet and marching away. "You insult me by talking such nonsense!"

"I didn’t mean to!" poor Alice pleaded. "You’re just so easily offended, you know!"

The Mouse only grumbled in reply.

"Please come back and finish your story!" Alice called after it, and the others all joined in chorus, "Yes, please do!"

But the Mouse only shook its head impatiently and walked even faster.

"What a shame it wouldn’t stay!" sighed the Lory as soon as the Mouse was completely out of sight.

An old Crab took the opportunity to lecture her daughter: "Ah, my dear! Let this be a lesson to you never to lose *your* temper!"

"Quiet, Mom!" snapped the young Crab. "You’d test the patience of an oyster!"

"I wish I had our Dinah here, I really do!" Alice said aloud, addressing nobody in particular. "She’d bring it back in no time!"

"And who is Dinah, if I might venture to ask?" inquired the Lory.

Alice answered eagerly, always ready to talk about her pet: "Dinah is our cat. And she’s so amazing at catching mice, you wouldn’t believe it! And oh, I wish you could see her go after birds! Why, she’ll gobble up a little bird as soon as look at it!"

This speech caused quite a stir among the group. Several birds hurried off immediately. An old Magpie began wrapping itself up very carefully, remarking, "I really must be heading home; the night air doesn’t agree with my throat!" A Canary called out in a trembling voice to its little ones, "Come along, sweethearts! It’s high time you were all in bed!" Under one excuse or another, they all slipped away, and Alice was soon left completely alone.

"I wish I hadn’t brought up Dinah!" she said to herself mournfully. "Nobody down here seems to like her, and I’m sure she’s the best cat in the whole world! Oh, my sweet Dinah! I wonder if I’ll ever see you again!"

And with that, poor Alice began to cry once more, feeling dreadfully lonely and discouraged. Before long, however, she heard the light patter of little footsteps in the distance. She looked up eagerly, half-hoping the Mouse had changed his mind and was coming back to finish his story.

# Chapter 4: The Rabbit Sends in a Little Bill

It was the White Rabbit, trotting slowly back again and looking around anxiously as it went, as if it had lost something. Alice heard it muttering to itself: "The Duchess! The Duchess! Oh, my dear paws! Oh, my fur and whiskers! She’ll have me executed, as sure as ferrets are ferrets! Where on earth could I have dropped them?"

Alice guessed in an instant that it was searching for the fan and the pair of white kid leather gloves. Being naturally good-natured, she began hunting around for them too, but they were nowhere to be found. Everything seemed to have changed since her swim in the pool of tears, and the grand hall with the glass table and the tiny door had vanished completely.

Before long, the Rabbit noticed Alice as she searched around, and barked at her in an angry tone, "Mary Ann! What *are* you doing out here? Run home this instant and fetch me a pair of gloves and a fan! Quick, now!"

Alice was so startled and frightened that she ran off at once in the direction it pointed, without even attempting to explain the mistake it had made.

"He thinks I’m his housemaid!" she said to herself as she ran. "How surprised he’ll be when he finds out who I really am! But I’d better take him his fan and gloves—that is, if I can find them."

Just as she said this, she came upon a neat little house. On the front door was a shiny brass plate engraved with the name "W. RABBIT." She walked in without knocking and hurried upstairs, terrified she might run into the real Mary Ann and be kicked out of the house before finding the fan and gloves.

"How strange it feels," Alice said to herself, "to be running errands for a rabbit! I suppose Dinah will be sending me on errands next!"

She began imagining how that would go:

"Miss Alice! Come here this instant and get ready for your walk!"
"Coming in a minute, Nanny! But I have to make sure the mouse doesn’t get out!"

"Only I don’t think," Alice went on, "they’d let Dinah stay in the house if she started bossing people around like that!"

By this time, she had found her way into a tidy little room with a table by the window. On it, just as she had hoped, lay a fan and two or three pairs of tiny white kid gloves. She picked up the fan and a pair of gloves and was just about to leave the room when her eyes caught a little bottle sitting near the mirror.

There was no label this time with the words "DRINK ME," but all the same, she uncorked it and brought it to her lips.

"I know *something* interesting always happens whenever I eat or drink anything down here," she told herself. "So I’ll just see what this bottle does. I really hope it makes me grow big again, because I’m thoroughly tired of being such a tiny little thing!"

It did indeed—and far faster than she had expected! Before she had even drunk half the bottle, her head was pressing against the ceiling, and she had to stoop to keep her neck from breaking.

She hastily set the bottle down, saying to herself, "That’s quite enough! I hope I won’t grow any more—as it is, I can’t even get out the door! I really wish I hadn’t drunk quite so much!"

Too late to wish that! She kept growing and growing, and very soon had to drop to her knees on the floor. Within another minute, there wasn’t even room for that, and she tried lying down with one elbow wedged against the door and her other arm curled around her head.

Still she went on growing! As a last resort, she stuck one arm out the window and thrust one foot up the chimney, telling herself, "Now I can’t do a single thing more, whatever happens. What *will* become of me?"

Luckily for Alice, the magic potion had now reached its full effect, and she grew no larger. Still, it was terribly uncomfortable, and since there seemed to be no chance whatever of ever getting out of the room again, it was no wonder she felt miserable.

"It was so much nicer at home," poor Alice thought, "when I wasn’t constantly growing bigger and smaller, and being bossed around by mice and rabbits. I almost wish I hadn’t gone down that rabbit hole—and yet... and yet... it really is rather fascinating, this kind of life! I do wonder what *could* have happened to me! Whenever I read fairy tales, I imagined that sort of thing never actually happened, and now here I am right in the middle of one! Someone ought to write a book about me—they really should! When I grow up, I’ll write one myself... but I’m already grown up now," she added sorrowfully. "At least, there’s no room to grow up any more *in here*!"

"Then again," thought Alice, "will I *never* get any older than I am right now? That would be a comfort, in a way—never having to be an old woman—but then, to always have school lessons to study! Oh, I wouldn’t like *that* at all!"

"Oh, you silly Alice!" she answered herself. "How could you do lessons in here? Why, there’s barely room for *you*, and no room at all for any textbooks!"

She went on like this, taking first one side and then the other, holding quite a lively conversation with herself. But after a few minutes, she heard a voice outside and stopped to listen.

"Mary Ann! Mary Ann!" shouted the voice. "Fetch me my gloves this instant!"

Then came the light patter of feet on the stairs. Alice knew it was the Rabbit coming to look for her, and she trembled until the whole house shook—completely forgetting that she was now about a thousand times larger than the Rabbit and had no reason at all to be afraid of it.

Soon the Rabbit came up to the door and tried to push it open. But since the door opened inward and Alice’s elbow was pressed hard against it, the attempt was a total failure.

Alice heard it say to itself, "Then I’ll go around and get in through the window."

"*That* you won’t!" thought Alice.

She waited until she imagined she heard the Rabbit right under the window, then suddenly opened her hand wide and made a quick snatch in the air. She didn’t catch anything, but she heard a little shriek, a thud, and a loud crash of shattered glass, from which she concluded it was quite likely he had tumbled into a cucumber frame or something similar.

Next came an angry voice—the Rabbit’s: "Pat! Pat! Where are you?"

Then a voice she had never heard before called back, "Sure then, I’m right here! Digging for apples, yer honor!"

"Digging for apples, my foot!" said the Rabbit furiously. "Come over here and help me out of *this*!" (Sounds of more glass crunching.)

"Now tell me, Pat, what’s that sticking out of the window?"

"Sure, it’s an arm, yer honor!" (He pronounced it "arr-um.")

"An arm, you goose! Who ever saw one that size? Why, it fills the whole window!"

"Sure it does, yer honor—but it’s an arm all the same!"

"Well, it has no business being there, at any rate! Go and take it away!"

A long silence followed, and Alice could only catch occasional whispers, such as:

"Sure, I don’t like the look of it, yer honor, not at all, at all!"
"Do as I tell you, you coward!"

Finally, Alice opened her hand again and made another snatch through the air. This time there were *two* little shrieks, followed by more crashing glass.

"What an awful lot of cucumber frames there must be!" thought Alice. "I wonder what they’ll try next! As for pulling me out through the window, I only wish they *could*! I certainly don’t want to stay stuck in here any longer!"

She waited for a good while without hearing anything more. At last came the rumble of little cartwheels and the sound of a large crowd of voices all talking at once. She could make out the words:

"Where’s the other ladder?"
"Why, I was only supposed to bring one! Bill’s got the other—"
"Bill! Bring it here, boy!"
"Here, set them up at this corner—"
"No, tie them together first—they don’t reach even halfway up yet!"
"Oh, they’ll do just fine; don’t be so picky!"
"Here, Bill! Grab hold of this rope—"
"Will the roof hold?"
"Watch that loose slate—look out, it’s slipping! Heads up below!" (A loud crash echoed outside.)
"Now, who did that?"
"It was Bill, I bet!"
"Who’s going down the chimney?"
"No way, not me! *You* do it!"
"I certainly won’t!"
"Bill’s going down! Here, Bill! The master says you’re to go down the chimney!"

"Oh! So Bill has to come down the chimney, does he?" Alice said to herself. "Why, they seem to dump everything onto Bill! I wouldn’t want to be in Bill’s shoes for anything. This fireplace is narrow, to be sure, but I *think* I can manage a kick!"

She reached her foot as far down into the chimney flue as she could and waited until she heard a little animal (she couldn’t tell what kind it was) scratching and scrambling around in the chimney just above her. Then, whispering to herself, "This is Bill," she gave one sharp, solid kick and waited to see what would happen next.

The first thing she heard was a general shout of, "There goes Bill!"

Then the Rabbit’s voice yelled, "Catch him, you by the hedge!"

Then came a moment of silence, followed by a flurry of confused voices:

"Hold up his head!"
"Give him some brandy!"
"Don’t choke him!"
"How was it, old pal? What happened to you? Tell us all about it!"

Finally came a feeble, squeaking little voice ("That’s Bill," thought Alice):

"Well, I hardly know... No more, thank you; I’m better now... but I’m far too shaken up to tell you! All I know is, something came at me like a jack-in-the-box, and up I went like a rocket!"

"You sure did, old pal!" the others agreed.

"We must burn the house down!" said the Rabbit’s voice.

At that, Alice yelled out as loud as she could, "If you do, I’ll set Dinah on you!"

An instant, dead silence fell over everything. Alice thought to herself, "I wonder what they *will* do next! If they had any sense at all, they’d take the roof off."

After a minute or two, they began moving around again, and Alice heard the Rabbit say, "A wheelbarrow full will do, to begin with."

"A wheelbarrow full of *what*?" Alice thought.

She didn’t have to wonder for long, because the next instant a shower of little pebbles came rattling through the window, and several of them hit her right in the face.

"I’ll put a stop to this!" she told herself, and shouted, "You’d better not do that again!"

Which produced another dead silence.

Alice noticed with surprise that the pebbles lying on the floor were all turning into little cakes, and a brilliant idea popped into her head.

"If I eat one of these cakes," she thought, "it’s bound to make *some* change in my size. And since it can’t possibly make me any larger, I suppose it must make me smaller!"

So she swallowed one of the cakes and was delighted to find that she began shrinking right away. As soon as she was small enough to get through the door, she dashed out of the house and found quite a crowd of small animals and birds waiting outside.

The poor little lizard, Bill, was in the middle, propped up by two guinea pigs who were feeding him something from a bottle.

They all rushed toward Alice the moment she appeared, but she ran off as fast as her legs could carry her and soon found herself safe in a dense wood.

"The first thing I have to do," Alice said to herself as she wandered through the woods, "is grow back to my proper size. And the second thing is to find my way into that lovely garden. I think that’s the best plan."

It sounded like an excellent plan, no doubt—very neat and simply arranged. The only trouble was that she didn’t have the slightest idea how to carry it out. And while she was peering anxiously among the trees, a sharp little bark right over her head made her look up in a hurry.

An enormous puppy was looking down at her with huge, round eyes, awkwardly stretching out one paw to touch her.

"Poor little thing!" Alice said coaxingly, trying hard to whistle to it. But all the while she was terribly frightened that it might be hungry, in which case it would probably gobble her right up, no matter how sweetly she spoke.

Hardly knowing what she was doing, she picked up a little stick and held it out toward the puppy.

Instantly, the puppy sprang into the air with all four paws off the ground, gave a yelp of pure delight, and charged at the stick, pretending to shake and chew it. Alice ducked behind a tall thistle to keep from being trampled. The moment she popped out on the other side, the puppy made another rush at the stick and tumbled head over heels in its eagerness to grab it.

Alice, thinking this was very much like playing a game of catch with a giant carthorse and expecting to get crushed under its paws at any second, darted around the thistle again. Then the puppy started a series of playful fake charges at the stick—darting forward a few inches, bounding backward several paces, and barking hoarsely the whole time—until at last it plopped down a little ways away, panting with its tongue lolling out of its mouth and its big eyes half shut.

This seemed like the perfect chance to escape. Alice took off at once, running until she was completely exhausted and out of breath, and until the puppy’s barks were only a faint sound in the distance.

"Still, what an adorable little puppy that was!" Alice said, leaning against a buttercup to catch her breath and fanning herself with one of its leaves. "I would have loved to teach it tricks—if only I’d been the right size to do it! Oh dear, I almost forgot that I need to grow back up again! Let’s see... how is that to be done? I suppose I ought to eat or drink something, but the big question is: what?"

The big question certainly was: what?

Alice looked all around at the flowers and the giant blades of grass, but she didn’t see anything that looked suitable to eat or drink under the circumstances.

There was a large mushroom growing nearby, roughly the same height as herself. After looking under it, around both sides of it, and behind it, it occurred to her that she might as well check to see what was on top.

She stretched up on tiptoe and peeked over the rim of the mushroom cap. Her eyes immediately met those of a large blue caterpillar, who was sitting on top with its arms folded, quietly smoking a long hookah pipe and paying not the slightest bit of attention to her or anyone else.

# Chapter 5: Advice from a Caterpillar

The Caterpillar and Alice looked at each other in silence for quite a while. At last, the Caterpillar took the hookah pipe out of its mouth and spoke to her in a slow, sleepy, drawling voice.

"Who are *you*?" asked the Caterpillar.

This wasn't exactly an encouraging start to a conversation. Alice replied rather shyly, "I—I honestly don't know right now, sir. At least, I know who I *was* when I woke up this morning, but I think I must have changed several times since then."

"What do you mean by that?" the Caterpillar asked sternly. "Explain yourself!"

"I'm afraid I can't explain *myself*, sir," Alice said, "because I'm not myself, you see."

"I don't see at all," said the Caterpillar.

"I'm afraid I can't put it any more clearly," Alice replied very politely, "because I don't understand it myself to begin with. And changing into so many different sizes in a single day is terribly confusing."

"No, it isn't," said the Caterpillar.

"Well, maybe you haven't experienced it yet," Alice said. "But when you have to turn into a chrysalis—and you will someday, you know—and then after that into a butterfly, I'd imagine you'll feel a bit strange, won't you?"

"Not one bit," said the Caterpillar.

"Well, maybe you feel differently," Alice said. "All I know is, it would feel very strange to *me*."

"You!" the Caterpillar said scornfully. "Who are *you*?"

That brought them right back to the beginning of the conversation. Alice was starting to feel rather annoyed by the Caterpillar's snappy, one-line answers. Drawing herself up, she said very seriously, "I think you ought to tell me who *you* are first."

"Why?" asked the Caterpillar.

Here was another baffling question. Since Alice couldn't think of a single good reason, and the Caterpillar seemed to be in an exceptionally foul mood, she turned to walk away.

"Come back!" the Caterpillar called after her. "I have something important to say!"

That certainly sounded promising. Alice turned around and walked back.

"Keep your temper," said the Caterpillar.

"Is that all?" Alice asked, swallowing down her annoyance as best as she could.

"No," said the Caterpillar.

Alice thought she might as well wait. She had nothing else to do, and maybe, just maybe, it would tell her something worth hearing. For a few minutes, it puffed away in silence. At last, it unfolded its arms, took the pipe from its mouth again, and asked, "So you think you've changed, do you?"

"I'm afraid so, sir," said Alice. "I can't remember things the way I used to—and I can't stay the same size for ten minutes in a row!"

"Can't remember *what* things?" asked the Caterpillar.

"Well, I tried to recite 'How doth the little busy bee,' but all the words came out totally wrong!" Alice replied in a mournful voice.

"Recite: '*You are old, Father William*,'" the Caterpillar commanded.

Alice folded her hands and began:

> "You are old, Father William," the young man said,  
> "And your hair has turned totally white;  
> Yet you constantly stand on your head upside down—  
> Do you think, at your age, that is right?"  
>  
> "In my youth," Father William replied to his son,  
> "I feared it might injure my brain;  
> But now that I'm perfectly sure I have none,  
> Why, I do it again and again!"  
>  
> "You are old," said the youth, "as I mentioned before,  
> And you've grown most remarkably fat;  
> Yet you flipped a back-somersault in through the door—  
> Now what is the reason for that?"  
>  
> "In my youth," said the sage, as he shook his gray locks,  
> "I kept all my joints loose and supple  
> By using this ointment—ten dollars a box—  
> Allow me to sell you a couple?"  
>  
> "You are old," said the youth, "and your jaws are too weak  
> For anything tougher than suet;  
> Yet you finished the goose, with the bones and the beak—  
> Just how did you manage to do it?"  
>  
> "In my youth," said his father, "I studied the law,  
> And argued each case with my wife;  
> And the muscular strength that it gave to my jaw  
> Has lasted the rest of my life."  
>  
> "You are old," said the youth, "one would hardly suppose  
> That your eye was as steady as ever;  
> Yet you balanced an eel on the tip of your nose—  
> What made you so terribly clever?"  
>  
> "I have answered three questions, and that is enough!"  
> Said his father. "Don't put on such airs!  
> Do you think I have all day to listen to stuff?  
> Get lost, or I'll kick you downstairs!"

"You didn't say that right," said the Caterpillar.

"Not *quite* right, I'm afraid," Alice admitted timidly. "Some of the words got mixed up."

"It's wrong from beginning to end," the Caterpillar said decisively. And with that, they sat in silence for several minutes.

The Caterpillar was the first to speak again.

"What size do you want to be?" it asked.

"Oh, I'm not that picky about my size," Alice quickly replied. "It's just that nobody likes changing so often, you know."

"I *don't* know," said the Caterpillar.

Alice didn't say anything. She had never been contradicted so much in all her life, and she could feel herself losing her patience.

"Are you satisfied now?" asked the Caterpillar.

"Well, I'd like to be a *little* taller, sir, if you don't mind," Alice said. "Three inches is such a miserable height to be."

"It is a perfectly fine height!" the Caterpillar snapped, rearing itself straight up as it spoke (it happened to be exactly three inches tall).

"But I'm not used to it!" poor Alice pleaded in a pitiful voice. And she thought to herself, *I wish these creatures wouldn't get offended so easily!*

"You'll get used to it in time," the Caterpillar said. It put the hookah pipe back in its mouth and began smoking again.

This time, Alice waited patiently until it felt like speaking. A minute or two later, the Caterpillar took the pipe out of its mouth, yawned once or twice, and shook itself all over. Then it climbed down off the mushroom and crawled away through the grass, casually remarking as it went, "One side will make you grow taller, and the other side will make you grow shorter."

*One side of what? The other side of what?* Alice thought to herself.

"Of the mushroom," said the Caterpillar, answering as if she had spoken out loud. A moment later, it was completely out of sight.

Alice stood staring thoughtfully at the mushroom for a minute, trying to figure out which were its two sides. Since it was perfectly round, she found this to be a rather tricky puzzle. Still, reaching her arms as far around it as they would go, she snapped off a piece of the edge with each hand.

"And now, which is which?" she murmured. She nibbled a tiny bite from the right-hand piece to test the effect. In the next instant, she felt a violent smack right under her chin—it had slammed straight into her foot!

The sudden change terrified her, but there was no time to lose—she was shrinking fast! She immediately set to work trying to nibble the other piece. Her chin was squished so tightly against her foot that there was barely enough room to open her mouth, but she managed it at last and swallowed a tiny crumb of the left-hand piece.

---

"Yes! My head's finally free!" Alice exclaimed with delight. But her joy instantly turned to alarm when she realized her shoulders were nowhere to be found. All she could see when she looked down was an impossibly long neck, rising like a giant stalk out of a sea of green leaves far below.

"What on earth *is* all that green stuff?" Alice wondered aloud. "And where *have* my shoulders gone? And oh, my poor hands, why can't I see you?" She waved them around as she spoke, but nothing seemed to happen, except for a faint rustling down in the distant canopy of leaves.

Since there seemed to be no hope of bringing her hands up to her head, she tried bringing her head down to her hands. To her delight, her neck bent easily in every direction, just like a snake. She had just managed to curve it into a graceful zigzag and was about to dive into the foliage—which turned out to be the treetops she had been wandering under—when a sharp hiss made her jerk backward. A large pigeon had flown right into her face and was furiously flapping its wings against her.

"Serpent!" screamed the Pigeon.

"I am *not* a serpent!" Alice said indignantly. "Leave me alone!"

"Serpent, I say!" the Pigeon repeated, dropping its voice to an exhausted, sobbing whisper. "I’ve tried everything, and nothing keeps them away!"

"I haven't the faintest idea what you're talking about," Alice said.

"I’ve tried the roots of trees, I’ve tried riverbanks, I’ve tried hedges," the Pigeon went on, paying zero attention to her. "But those serpents! There’s simply no pleasing them!"

Alice grew more and more baffled, but she figured there was no point saying anything else until the Pigeon had finished venting.

"As if hatching eggs wasn't hard enough work already!" the Pigeon complained. "Now I have to keep watch for serpents day and night! I haven't slept a wink in three weeks!"

"I'm so sorry you've been having such a hard time," Alice said, starting to understand.

"And just when I picked the tallest tree in the entire woods," the Pigeon continued, its voice ramping up to a screech, "and just when I thought I was finally safe from them, they have to come slithering down from the sky! Ugh, you serpent!"

"But I'm *not* a serpent, I tell you!" Alice cried. "I'm a... I'm a..."

"Well? *What* are you?" demanded the Pigeon. "I can tell you’re trying to make something up!"

"I—I'm a little girl," Alice said rather hesitantly, remembering just how many changes she had gone through that day.

"A likely story!" scoffed the Pigeon with utter contempt. "I’ve seen plenty of little girls in my time, but not *one* with a neck like that! No, no! You’re a serpent, and there’s no point denying it. I suppose you'll tell me next that you've never tasted an egg!"

"I *have* eaten eggs, of course," said Alice, who was a very honest child. "Little girls eat eggs just as much as serpents do, you know."

"I don’t believe it," said the Pigeon. "And even if they do, that just means they’re a kind of serpent too, as far as I'm concerned."

This was such a startling new perspective that Alice stayed completely silent for a minute or two. That gave the Pigeon the chance to add, "You’re hunting for eggs, I know *that* much. And what difference does it make to me whether you’re a little girl or a serpent?"

"It makes a big difference to *me*," Alice said quickly. "Besides, I’m not looking for eggs at all. And even if I were, I wouldn’t want *yours*—I don’t like raw eggs!"

"Well, get lost then!" the Pigeon snapped sulkily, settling back down into its nest.

Alice ducked down among the trees as best as she could. Her neck kept getting tangled in the branches, forcing her to stop every few moments to untangle herself. After a while, she remembered she was still holding the pieces of mushroom. Working very carefully, she nibbled first on one piece and then on the other, shooting up and shrinking down, until at last she managed to get herself back to her normal height.

It had been so long since she had been anywhere near her regular size that it felt bizarre at first. But after a few minutes she adjusted and started talking to herself again, as usual.

"Well, that’s half my plan accomplished! All these transformations are so baffling—I never know what I’m going to look like from one minute to the next! Still, I’m back to my normal size. The next step is getting into that gorgeous garden. I wonder how in the world I’m going to do that?"

Just as she said this, she stepped out into a clearing with a little house about four feet tall.

*Whoever lives in there,* Alice thought, *it will never do to barge in at this size. I’d scare them half to death!*

So she began nibbling on the right-hand piece of mushroom again, refusing to go anywhere near the house until she had shrunk herself down to just nine inches tall.

# Chapter 6: Pig and Pepper

For a minute or two, Alice stood looking at the house, wondering what to do next. Suddenly, a footman in fancy uniform came running out of the woods. (She assumed he was a footman because of the uniform; otherwise, judging purely by his face, she would have taken him for a fish.) He rapped loudly on the door with his knuckles.

The door was opened by another footman in uniform, who had a round face and huge, bulging frog eyes. Alice noticed that both footmen had powdered wigs that curled all over their heads. Overcome with curiosity to know what was going on, she crept a little way out from the woods to listen.

The Fish-Footman began by pulling an enormous letter from under his arm—it was nearly as big as he was—and handed it over to the other, announcing in a solemn voice, "For the Duchess. An invitation from the Queen to play croquet."

The Frog-Footman repeated in the exact same grave tone, merely flipping the order of the words around: "From the Queen. An invitation for the Duchess to play croquet."

Then both of them bowed so low that their powdered curls got hopelessly tangled together.

Alice burst out laughing at this and had to duck back into the trees for fear they would hear her. When she peeked out again, the Fish-Footman was gone, and the Frog-Footman was sitting on the ground near the door, staring blankly up at the sky.

Alice walked timidly up to the door and knocked.

"There’s not the slightest use in knocking," the Footman remarked, "and that's for two reasons. First, because I’m on the same side of the door as you are. Second, because they’re making such a racket inside that nobody could possibly hear you."

And there certainly *was* an extraordinary racket going on inside: a nonstop chorus of howling and sneezing, punctuated every few seconds by a tremendous crash, as if another dish or kettle had been smashed to pieces.

"Then please," said Alice, "how am I supposed to get in?"

"There might be some sense in your knocking," the Footman went on, ignoring her completely, "if we had the door between us. For instance, if you were *inside*, you could knock, and I could let you out."

He kept staring straight up into the sky the entire time he spoke, which Alice thought was downright rude. *Maybe he can’t help it,* she thought to herself. *His eyes are practically on top of his head. Still, he could at least answer a question.*

"How am I supposed to get in?" she repeated, louder this time.

"I’m going to sit right here," the Footman observed, "until tomorrow—"

At that exact moment, the front door flew open and a large plate came skimming out, straight at the Footman’s head. It grazed his nose and shattered to pieces against a tree behind him.

"—or the next day, maybe," the Footman continued in the exact same tone, just as if nothing had happened.

"How am I to get in?" Alice asked again, raising her voice.

"*Are* you supposed to get in at all?" said the Footman. "That’s the first question, you know."

It certainly was, but Alice hated being told so. "It’s really maddening," she muttered to herself, "the way every creature here argues. It’s enough to drive anyone crazy!"

The Footman apparently thought this was an ideal moment to repeat himself, with variations. "I shall sit here," he said, "on and off, for days and days."

"But what am *I* supposed to do?" asked Alice.

"Whatever you like," said the Footman, and began whistling.

"Oh, it's completely useless talking to him," Alice groaned in frustration. "He's a total idiot!"

And with that, she opened the door and walked right in.

The door led straight into a large kitchen, which was thick with smoke from one end to the other. In the middle of the room, the Duchess sat on a three-legged stool nursing a baby. Over by the fire, the cook was leaning over a massive cauldron, stirring what looked like a giant batch of soup.

*There is definitely way too much pepper in that soup!* Alice thought to herself, coughing and sneezing as best as she could speak.

There was certainly way too much of it in the air. Even the Duchess sneezed every now and then; and as for the baby, it took turns sneezing and howling at the top of its lungs without a single second's pause. The only two beings in the entire kitchen who weren't sneezing were the cook and a large cat sitting by the hearth, grinning from ear to ear.

"Excuse me, could you please tell me," Alice asked rather timidly—she wasn't quite sure whether good manners allowed her to speak first—"why your cat grins like that?"

"It's a Cheshire Cat," said the Duchess, "and that's why. Pig!"

She shouted that last word with such sudden violence that Alice jumped in surprise. But in the next second, Alice realized the insult was aimed at the baby, not at her. Gathering her courage, she tried again:

"I didn't know Cheshire cats always grinned. In fact, I didn't know cats *could* grin."

"Every cat can," said the Duchess, "and most of them do."

"I don't know of any that do," Alice said very politely, quite pleased to have struck up a conversation.

"You don't know much," snapped the Duchess, "and that's a fact."

Alice didn't like the tone of that remark at all, and decided it would be wise to change the subject. But while she was trying to think of one, the cook hauled the cauldron of soup off the fire and immediately began hurling everything within arm's reach at the Duchess and the baby.

First came the fire irons—the tongs and the poker—followed by a storm of saucepans, plates, and dishes. The Duchess paid them no attention whatsoever, even when they struck her directly. As for the baby, it was already howling so loudly that it was impossible to tell whether the blows hurt it or not.

"Oh, *please* watch what you're doing!" cried Alice, jumping up and down in pure terror. "Oh, watch out—there goes his *poor* little nose!" she shrieked, as an unusually large saucepan whizzed past the baby, very nearly taking its nose clean off.

"If everybody just minded their own business," the Duchess growled hoarsely, "the world would spin a whole lot faster than it does."

"Which would *not* be a good thing," said Alice, thrilled for an opportunity to show off a little of her knowledge. "Just think of the chaos it would create with day and night! You see, the Earth takes twenty-four hours to rotate on its axis—"

"Speaking of axes," the Duchess interrupted, "chop off her head!"

Alice glanced anxiously over at the cook to see if she intended to follow the order. But the cook was busily stirring the soup and didn't seem to be listening. Alice pressed on: "Twenty-four hours, I *think*... or is it twelve? I—"

"Oh, don't bother *me*!" the Duchess groaned. "I've never been able to stand numbers!"

With that, she went back to nursing her baby, singing a strange sort of lullaby to it and giving the child a violent shake at the end of every line:

> "Speak roughly to your little boy,  
> And smack him when he sneezes;  
> He only does it to annoy,  
> Because he knows it teases!"  
>  
> **Chorus**  
> (joined by the cook and the baby):  
>  
> "Wow! Wow! Wow!"  

While the Duchess sang the second verse, she kept tossing the baby violently up and down in the air. The poor little thing was wailing so loudly that Alice could barely make out the words:

> "I speak severely to my boy,  
> I smack him when he sneezes;  
> For he can totally enjoy  
> The pepper when he pleases!"  
>  
> **Chorus**:  
>  
> "Wow! Wow! Wow!"  

"Here! You can hold him for a bit if you want!" the Duchess shouted at Alice, tossing the baby straight at her. "I have to go get ready to play croquet with the Queen!"

With that, she rushed out of the room. The cook threw a frying pan after her as she went, but it just missed.

Alice caught the baby with no small amount of difficulty. It was an oddly shaped little creature that kept sticking its arms and legs out in every direction at once. *Just like a starfish,* Alice thought. The poor little thing was snorting like a steam engine when she caught it, constantly curling into a tight ball and then springing straight out again. For the first minute or two, it was all she could do just to keep a grip on it.

As soon as she figured out the trick to holding it—which was to fold it into a sort of knot and keep a tight grip on its right ear and left foot so it couldn't untangle itself—she carried it out into the fresh open air.

*If I don't take this child with me,* Alice thought, *they're bound to kill it in a day or two. Leaving it behind would be practically murder!*

She said those last words out loud, and the little creature gave a grunt in reply (it had finally stopped sneezing by now).

"Don't grunt," Alice said. "That's no proper way to express yourself."

The baby grunted again, and Alice looked anxiously into its face to see what was wrong. There could be no mistake about it: it had an exceptionally upturned nose, looking far more like a snout than a human nose. Its eyes were also turning remarkably small for a baby. All in all, Alice didn't like the look of it one bit.

*Maybe it was just sobbing,* she thought, and looked closely into its eyes again to see if there were any tears.

Nope, not a single tear.

"If you're going to turn into a pig, my dear," Alice said seriously, "I won't have anything more to do with you. Consider yourself warned!"

The poor little creature sobbed again—or grunted, it was impossible to tell which—and they walked along together in silence for a while.

Alice was just beginning to wonder, *Now, what in the world am I going to do with this creature when I get it home?* when it let out another grunt—so loud and violent that she looked down into its face in alarm.

This time, there could be *no* mistake about it: it was neither more nor less than a pig. Carrying it any farther would be completely absurd.

So she set the little creature down on the ground, feeling immense relief as she watched it trot peacefully away into the woods.

"If it had grown up," Alice said to herself, "it would have made a dreadfully ugly child. But it makes rather a handsome pig, I think."

She started thinking about several other children she knew who might make very fine pigs, and was just musing to herself, *If only people knew the right trick to transform them—* when she jumped in surprise. There was the Cheshire Cat, sitting on the branch of a tree just a few yards away.

The Cat merely grinned when it saw Alice. It looked good-natured, she thought, but it also had *very* long claws and an awful lot of teeth, so she felt it ought to be treated with respect.

"Cheshire Puss," she began rather timidly, not knowing at all whether it would like the name. The Cat only stretched its grin a little wider.

*Well, it seems pleased so far,* thought Alice, and she went on: "Would you please tell me which way I ought to go from here?"

"That depends a good deal on where you want to get to," said the Cat.

"I don't much care where—" said Alice.

"Then it doesn't matter which way you go," said the Cat.

"—as long as I get *somewhere*," Alice added by way of explanation.

"Oh, you're bound to do that," said the Cat, "if you only walk long enough."

Alice had to admit there was no arguing with that logic, so she tried another question: "What kind of people live around here?"

"In *that* direction," the Cat said, waving its right paw in a circle, "lives a Hatter. And in *that* direction," waving the other paw, "lives a March Hare. Visit whichever one you like—they're both completely mad."

"But I don't want to go among mad people," Alice objected.

"Oh, you can't help that," said the Cat. "We're all mad here. I'm mad. You're mad."

"How do you know I'm mad?" asked Alice.

"You must be," said the Cat, "or you wouldn't have come here."

Alice didn't think that proved anything at all, but she pressed on: "And how do you know that *you're* mad?"

"To begin with," said the Cat, "a dog isn't mad. You grant that?"

"I suppose so," said Alice.

"Well, then," the Cat went on, "you see, a dog growls when it's angry, and wags its tail when it's pleased. Now, *I* growl when I'm pleased, and wag my tail when I'm angry. Therefore, I'm mad."

"*I* call that purring, not growling," said Alice.

"Call it whatever you like," said the Cat. "Are you playing croquet with the Queen today?"

"I'd love to very much," said Alice, "but I haven't been invited yet."

"You'll see me there," said the Cat—and vanished.

Alice wasn't even especially surprised by this; she was getting so used to strange things happening. While she was still staring at the spot where it had been, it suddenly popped back into view.

"By the way, what ever became of the baby?" the Cat asked. "I almost forgot to ask."

"It turned into a pig," Alice answered calmly, speaking as casually as if the Cat had simply strolled back into the room.

"Thought it would," said the Cat, and vanished again.

Alice waited a little while, half-expecting to see it again, but it didn't appear. After a minute or two, she started walking in the direction where the March Hare was said to live.

"I've seen hatters before," she told herself. "The March Hare will be much more interesting. And since it's May, maybe it won't be raving mad—at least not as crazy as it was in March."

Just as she said this, she looked up, and there was the Cat again, perched comfortably on a tree branch.

"Did you say *pig*, or *fig*?" asked the Cat.

"I said *pig*," replied Alice. "And I really wish you wouldn't keep appearing and vanishing so suddenly! You make my head spin!"

"All right," said the Cat.

This time, it vanished very slowly, starting at the tip of its tail and ending with its grin, which lingered floating in the air for quite a while after the rest of its body was gone.

"Well!" thought Alice. "I've often seen a cat without a grin, but a grin without a cat! That is the most curious thing I ever saw in all my life!"

She hadn't gone much farther before she came in sight of the March Hare's house. She was pretty sure it was the right place, because the chimneys were shaped like rabbit ears and the roof was thatched with fur. It was such a large house that she didn't want to get any closer until she took another nibble from the left-hand piece of mushroom, growing herself up to about two feet tall. Even then, she walked up toward it rather timidly, saying to herself, "What if it really is raving mad after all? I almost wish I'd gone to see the Hatter instead!"

# Chapter 7: A Mad Tea-Party

A table was set out under a tree in front of the house, where the March Hare and the Hatter were having tea. A Dormouse was sitting between them, fast asleep, and the other two were using it as a cushion—resting their elbows on it and talking right over its head.

"That must be so uncomfortable for the Dormouse," Alice thought. "Though I suppose as long as it's asleep, it doesn't really mind."

The table was enormous, but the three of them were crammed together at one corner.

"No room! No room!" they shouted the moment they saw Alice walking over.

"There's *plenty* of room!" Alice said indignantly. She pulled up a large armchair at the end of the table and sat down.

"Have some wine," the March Hare offered in an encouraging voice.

Alice looked all around the table, but there was nothing on it except tea. "I don't see any wine," she remarked.

"There isn't any," said the March Hare.

"Then it wasn't very polite of you to offer it," Alice said crossly.

"It wasn't very polite of you to sit down without being invited," shot back the March Hare.

"I didn't know it was *your* table," said Alice. "It's set for way more than three people."

"Your hair needs cutting," said the Hatter. He had been staring at Alice for some time with intense curiosity, and these were the very first words out of his mouth.

"You really should learn not to make personal remarks," Alice said sternly. "It's very rude."

The Hatter's eyes went wide at that, but all he said was, "Why is a raven like a writing desk?"

"Oh, good, now we're going to have some fun!" thought Alice. "I'm glad they've started with riddles."

"I think I can guess that one," she added aloud.

"Do you mean you think you can figure out the answer?" asked the March Hare.

"Exactly," said Alice.

"Then you should say what you mean," the March Hare pressed on.

"I do," Alice replied quickly. "At least—well, at least I mean what I say. That's the same thing, isn't it?"

"Not even close to the same thing!" said the Hatter. "You might just as well say that 'I see what I eat' is the same thing as 'I eat what I see'!"

"You might just as well say," added the March Hare, "that 'I like what I get' is the same thing as 'I get what I like'!"

"You might just as well say," chimed in the Dormouse, who seemed to be talking in his sleep, "that 'I breathe when I sleep' is the same thing as 'I sleep when I breathe'!"

"Well, in *your* case it actually is the same thing," said the Hatter.

With that, the conversation died, and the group sat in silence for a minute while Alice racked her brain for everything she knew about ravens and writing desks—which wasn't very much at all.

The Hatter was the first to break the silence. "What's the date today?" he asked, turning to Alice. He had pulled a pocket watch from his waistcoat and was looking at it anxiously, giving it an occasional shake and holding it up to his ear.

Alice thought for a moment. "The fourth."

"Two days off!" sighed the Hatter. "I told you butter wouldn't work on the gears!" He glared angrily at the March Hare.

"It was the *best* butter," the March Hare meekly replied.

"Yes, but crumbs must have gotten inside too," the Hatter grumbled. "You shouldn't have smeared it in with a bread knife."

The March Hare took the watch and inspected it gloomily. He dunked it right into his cup of tea, pulled it out, and looked at it again, but he couldn't think of anything better to say than his first defense: "It really was the *best* butter, you know."

Alice had been peering over his shoulder with curiosity. "What a funny watch!" she remarked. "It tells the day of the month, but it doesn't tell what time it is!"

"Why should it?" muttered the Hatter. "Does *your* watch tell you what year it is?"

"Of course not," Alice answered immediately. "Because it stays the same year for such a long time."

"Which is exactly the case with *mine*," said the Hatter.

Alice felt thoroughly baffled. The Hatter's remark didn't seem to make any sense whatsoever, and yet it was definitely English. "I don't quite understand you," she said, as politely as she could manage.

"The Dormouse is asleep again," the Hatter noted, and he poured a splash of hot tea right onto its nose.

The Dormouse shook its head in annoyance and mumbled, without even opening its eyes, "Of course, of course. Exactly what I was about to remark myself."

"Solved the riddle yet?" the Hatter asked, turning back to Alice.

"No, I give up," Alice replied. "What's the answer?"

"I haven't the slightest idea," said the Hatter.

"Me neither," said the March Hare.

Alice let out an exasperated sigh. "I think you could do something better with your time," she said, "than waste it asking riddles with no answers."

"If you knew Time as well as I do," said the Hatter, "you wouldn't talk about wasting *it*. It's *him*."

"I don't understand what you mean," said Alice.

"Of course you don't!" the Hatter snapped, tossing his head dismissively. "I bet you've never even spoken to Time!"

"Maybe not," Alice replied cautiously. "Though I know I have to keep time when I practice music."

"Ah! That explains it," said the Hatter. "He can't stand being beaten! Now, if you just stayed on good terms with him, he'd do practically anything you wanted with the clock. For instance, suppose it's nine in the morning, right when lessons start: all you'd have to do is drop a quiet whisper to Time, and zip! The hands spin around in a flash. One-thirty—time for lunch!"

("I only wish it were," the March Hare muttered to himself in a whisper.)

"That would be amazing, definitely," Alice said thoughtfully. "Except—well, I wouldn't actually be hungry yet, you know."

"Maybe not at first," said the Hatter. "But you could keep it at one-thirty for as long as you wanted."

"Is that how *you* manage it?" Alice asked.

The Hatter shook his head sorrowfully. "Not me!" he replied. "We had a falling-out last March—right before *he* went completely mad, you know—" (he pointed his teaspoon at the March Hare) "—it happened at the grand concert hosted by the Queen of Hearts. I had to sing:

> "Twinkle, twinkle, little bat!  
> How I wonder what you're at!"

Maybe you know the song?"

"I've heard something like it," said Alice.

"It keeps going, you see," the Hatter continued, "like this:

> "Up above the world you fly,  
> Like a tea-tray in the sky.  
> Twinkle, twinkle—"

At this, the Dormouse twitched and started singing in its sleep: "*Twinkle, twinkle, twinkle, twinkle—*" and went on for so long that they had to pinch it hard to make it stop.

"Well, I'd barely finished the first verse," the Hatter said, "when the Queen leaped up and screamed, 'He's murdering the time! Off with his head!'"

"How dreadfully savage!" Alice gasped.

"And ever since then," the Hatter went on in a mournful tone, "Time won't do a single thing I ask. It's always six o'clock now."

A bright idea suddenly hit Alice. "Is that why so many tea things are set out here?" she asked.

"Yes, exactly," sighed the Hatter. "It's always teatime, and we never have any time to wash the dishes in between."

"So you just keep moving around the table, I suppose?" said Alice.

"Precisely," said the Hatter. "As things get used up."

"But what happens when you get back to where you started?" Alice dared to ask.

"How about we change the subject?" the March Hare interrupted with a wide yawn. "I'm getting tired of this. I vote the young lady tells us a story."

"I'm afraid I don't know any," Alice said, rather panicked by the suggestion.

"Then the Dormouse will!" they both cheered. "Wake up, Dormouse!" And they pinched him from both sides at the exact same time.

The Dormouse slowly cracked his eyes open. "I wasn't asleep," he rasped in a faint, groggy voice. "I heard every single word you guys said."

"Tell us a story!" said the March Hare.

"Yes, please do!" Alice pleaded.

"And make it quick," added the Hatter, "or you'll fall asleep again before you're done."

"Once upon a time, there were three little sisters," the Dormouse rushed to begin, "and their names were Elsie, Lacie, and Tillie. And they lived at the bottom of a well—"

"What did they eat to stay alive?" asked Alice, who always took a deep interest in matters of food and drink.

"They lived on syrup," said the Dormouse, after thinking for a minute or two.

"They couldn't possibly have done that, you know," Alice pointed out gently. "They would have gotten dreadfully sick."

"And so they did," said the Dormouse. "*Very* sick."

Alice tried to imagine what such a bizarre way of living would be like, but it was just too baffling. So she continued: "But why did they live at the bottom of a well?"

"Have some more tea," the March Hare offered Alice, very earnestly.

"I haven't had any yet," Alice replied in an offended tone, "so I can't have *more*."

"You mean you can't have *less*," said the Hatter. "It's remarkably easy to have *more* than nothing."

"Nobody asked for *your* opinion," snapped Alice.

"Now who's making personal remarks?" the Hatter asked triumphantly.

Alice didn't quite know what to say to that. Instead, she helped herself to some tea and bread and butter, then turned back to the Dormouse and repeated her question: "Why did they live at the bottom of a well?"

The Dormouse took another minute or two to mull it over, and then said, "It was a syrup well."

"There's no such thing!" Alice started to say angrily, but the Hatter and the March Hare shushed her loudly: "Shh! Shh!"

The Dormouse sulked and mumbled, "If you can't be polite, you can just finish the story yourself."

"No, please go on!" Alice apologized humbly. "I won't interrupt again. I suppose maybe there could be *one*."

"One, honestly!" sniffed the Dormouse with indignation. Even so, he agreed to continue. "And so these three little sisters—they were learning how to draw, you know—"

"What did they draw?" Alice asked, completely forgetting her promise.

"Syrup," said the Dormouse, without pausing for even a second this time.

"I want a clean cup," the Hatter interrupted. "Everyone shift down one seat!"

He slid over as he spoke, and the Dormouse followed him. The March Hare moved into the Dormouse's spot, and Alice reluctantly took the March Hare's seat. The Hatter was the only one who gained anything from the shuffle. Alice ended up far worse off than before, since the March Hare had just tipped over the milk jug right onto his plate.

Alice didn't want to upset the Dormouse again, so she proceeded very carefully. "I still don't quite understand. Where did they draw the syrup from?"

"You draw water out of a water well," said the Hatter, "so I'd think you could draw syrup out of a syrup well—right, genius?"

"But they were *inside* the well," Alice said to the Dormouse, choosing to ignore the Hatter's insult.

"Of course they were," said the Dormouse. "*Well* inside."

This answer left poor Alice so thoroughly confused that she let the Dormouse go on for a good while without interrupting.

"They were learning how to draw," the Dormouse continued, yawning and rubbing his eyes, for he was growing terribly sleepy, "and they drew all kinds of things—anything that begins with the letter M—"

"Why with an M?" asked Alice.

"Why not?" retorted the March Hare.

Alice stayed silent.

By this time, the Dormouse had shut his eyes and was drifting off to sleep. But with a sharp pinch from the Hatter, he woke back up with a little shriek and rattled on: "—that begins with an M, like mousetraps, and the moon, and memories, and muchness—you know how people say things are 'much of a muchness'? Did you ever see a drawing of a muchness?"

"Honestly, now that you ask me," said Alice, utterly confused, "I don't think—"

"Then you shouldn't talk," snapped the Hatter.

This final insult was more than Alice could bear. Thoroughly disgusted, she stood up and walked away. The Dormouse fell asleep instantly, and neither of the other two paid the slightest bit of attention to her departure, though she glanced back once or twice, half hoping they might call after her. The last glimpse she caught of them, they were trying to stuff the sleeping Dormouse headfirst into the teapot.

"I am never going back *there* again!" Alice declared as she picked her path through the woods. "That was the stupidest tea party I've ever been to in my entire life!"

Just as she muttered this, she noticed that one of the trees had a door leading right into the trunk.

"Well, that's curious!" she thought. "Then again, everything is curious today. Might as well go in right away."

And in she went.

Once again, she found herself standing in the long hallway, right beside the little glass table.

"All right, I'll handle this much better this time," she told herself.

She began by taking the little golden key and unlocking the door that led out into the garden. Then she went to work nibbling on the mushroom—she had kept a piece tucked in her pocket—until she shrank down to about a foot tall. Then she walked down the narrow passageway, and *then*—at long last, there she was, stepping out into the gorgeous garden, surrounded by vibrant flower beds and cool, refreshing fountains.

# Chapter 8: The Queen's Croquet-Ground

A large rose bush stood near the entrance of the garden. Its roses were naturally white, but three gardeners were busily brushing red paint over them. Alice thought this was an extraordinarily strange sight, so she walked closer to watch. Just as she approached, she heard one of them yell, "Watch out, Five! Stop splashing paint all over me like that!"

"I couldn't help it," Five muttered sulkily. "Seven bumped my elbow."

Seven looked up and snapped, "That's it, Five! Always blame someone else!"

"*You* shouldn't be talking!" said Five. "Just yesterday I heard the Queen say you deserved to be beheaded!"

"What for?" asked the gardener who had spoken first.

"That's none of *your* business, Two!" Seven barked.

"Yes, it *is* his business!" countered Five. "And I'll tell him—it was for bringing the cook tulip bulbs instead of onions!"

Seven threw down his paintbrush and started, "Well, of all the unfair—" when his eyes happened to land on Alice standing right there watching them. He stopped dead in his tracks. The other two spun around as well, and all three instantly bowed low to the ground.

"Could you tell me," Alice asked a little timidly, "why you are painting those roses?"

Five and Seven said nothing, looking nervously at Two. Two cleared his throat and said in a low voice, "Well, the truth is, miss, this was supposed to be a *red* rose bush, and we planted a white one by mistake. If the Queen finds out, every one of us will have our heads chopped off. So you see, miss, we're doing our absolute best before she gets here to—"

At that very moment Five, who had been anxiously scanning across the garden, shrieked, "The Queen! The Queen!"

The three gardeners instantly threw themselves flat on their faces on the grass. The sound of numerous marching footsteps drew near, and Alice turned around, eager to catch sight of the Queen.

First marched ten soldiers carrying clubs. Like the three gardeners, they were rectangular and completely flat, with their hands and feet poking out from the corners. Next came ten courtiers, decorated all over with diamond suits, marching two by two just like the soldiers. Behind them skipped the royal children—ten of them, bouncing along hand in hand in pairs, all adorned with hearts. Next came the royal guests, mostly visiting Kings and Queens, and among them Alice recognized the White Rabbit. He was chattering in a fast, nervous flutter, smiling at every single word that was said, and hurried right past without noticing her at all. Then followed the Knave of Hearts, carrying the King's golden crown on a plush crimson velvet cushion. And bringing up the rear of the grand procession came THE KING AND QUEEN OF HEARTS.

Alice wondered whether she ought to throw herself face-down on the ground like the three gardeners, but she couldn't remember ever hearing of any rule like that for parades.

"Besides," she thought, "what would be the point of having a parade if everyone had to lie face-down and couldn't even watch it?"

So she stood her ground right where she was and waited.

When the procession drew level with Alice, everyone halted and stared at her.

"Who is this?" the Queen demanded severely. She asked the question of the Knave of Hearts, who only bowed and smiled in response.

"Idiot!" snapped the Queen, tossing her head in annoyance. Turning toward Alice, she barked, "What's your name, child?"

"My name is Alice, if it please Your Majesty," Alice replied very politely. But in her head, she added, *Wait, they're only a deck of cards, after all. There's no reason to be afraid of them!*

"And who are *these?*" the Queen demanded, pointing down at the three gardeners sprawled around the rose bush. Since they were lying on their faces and the pattern on their backs matched the rest of the deck, she had no idea whether they were gardeners, soldiers, courtiers, or three of her own children.

"How should *I* know?" Alice replied, surprising herself with her own boldness. "It's none of *my* business."

The Queen flushed crimson with fury. Glaring at Alice like a wild beast, she screeched, "Off with her head! Off—"

"Nonsense!" Alice shouted, so loudly and decisively that the Queen went completely silent.

The King laid a timid hand on the Queen's arm. "Consider, my dear," he whispered nervously. "She is only a child!"

The Queen jerked away from him angrily and snapped at the Knave, "Turn them over!"

The Knave did so, very carefully, using the tip of his shoe.

"Get up!" the Queen ordered in a piercing, shrill voice.

The three gardeners leaped up instantly and began bowing furiously to the King, the Queen, the royal children, and everyone else in sight.

"Stop that!" screamed the Queen. "You're making me dizzy!" She wheeled toward the rose bush. "What *have* you been doing here?"

"If it please Your Majesty," Two stammered in a humble voice, dropping onto one knee, "we were trying—"

"*I* see!" snapped the Queen, who had been inspecting the freshly painted roses. "Off with their heads!"

The procession began moving forward again, leaving three soldiers behind to execute the unfortunate gardeners. In complete panic, the gardeners rushed over to Alice for protection.

"You won't be beheaded!" Alice promised them, quickly scooping them up and tucking them into a large empty flowerpot nearby.

The three soldiers wandered around for a minute or two searching for them, gave up, and quietly marched off to rejoin the group.

"Are their heads off?" the Queen shouted back.

"Their heads are gone, if it please Your Majesty!" the soldiers yelled in reply.

"Good!" the Queen shouted. "Do you play croquet?"

The soldiers fell silent and looked at Alice, as the question was obviously meant for her.

"Yes!" shouted Alice.

"Come on, then!" roared the Queen.

Alice joined the procession, wondering with intense curiosity what would happen next.

"It's... it's a very fine day, isn't it?" said a timid voice beside her.

She looked over to find the White Rabbit walking right at her side, peeking anxiously up into her face.

"Very," said Alice. "Where's the Duchess?"

"Hush! Quiet!" whispered the Rabbit in a hurried, terrified tone. He glanced nervously over his shoulder, stood on his tiptoes to reach her ear, and whispered, "She's been sentenced to execution."

"What for?" asked Alice.

"Did you say 'What a pity'?" the Rabbit asked.

"No, I didn't," said Alice. "I don't think it's a pity at all. I asked, 'What for?'"

"She boxed the Queen's ears—" the Rabbit began.

Alice let out an involuntary gasp of laughter.

"Oh, hush!" the Rabbit squeaked in terror. "The Queen will hear you! You see, the Duchess showed up rather late, and the Queen said—"

"GET TO YOUR PLACES!" the Queen bellowed in a voice like thunder.

Instantly, people went scrambling in every direction, tripping and tumbling over one another. Within a minute or two, however, everyone got sorted out and the game officially began.

Alice thought she had never seen such a bizarre croquet court in her entire life. The ground was all bumpy ridges and deep furrows. The croquet balls were live hedgehogs, the mallets were live flamingos, and the soldiers had to bend over backward on their hands and feet to form the arches!

Alice's biggest struggle at first was figuring out how to handle her flamingo. She managed to tuck its body fairly comfortably under her arm, with its long legs dangling down. But almost every time she got its neck nicely straightened out and was just about to whack the hedgehog with its head, the bird would twist its long neck around and stare straight into her face with such an utterly bewildered expression that she couldn't help bursting out laughing. Then, once she managed to wrestle its head back down to try again, she'd discover to her extreme frustration that the hedgehog had unrolled itself and was in the middle of crawling away! On top of that, there was almost always a mound or a ditch in the way wherever she wanted to send the hedgehog, and since the arched card soldiers were constantly standing up and wandering off to different spots across the lawn, Alice soon came to the conclusion that this was an extraordinarily difficult game.

The players all played at the exact same time without waiting for turns, bickering constantly and wrestling over hedgehogs. In no time at all, the Queen was flying into a blind rage, stomping around the lawn and screaming, "Off with his head!" or "Off with her head!" about once every minute.

Alice began to feel deeply uneasy. True, she hadn't gotten into a dispute with the Queen yet, but she knew it could happen at any second.

*And then,* thought Alice, *what will become of me? They are dreadfully fond of beheading people around here. The real wonder is that anyone is still left alive!*

She was looking around for a way to escape, wondering if she could sneak off without being spotted, when she noticed a curious phenomenon in the air. It puzzled her at first, but after watching it for a minute or two, she made it out to be a wide grin.

"It's the Cheshire Cat!" she said to herself. "Now I'll finally have someone to talk to."

"How are you getting along?" the Cat asked, as soon as enough of its mouth appeared to speak.

Alice waited until the eyes materialized, and then nodded. *There's no point talking to it,* she thought, *until its ears show up—or at least one of them.*

Within another minute the entire head appeared. Alice set down her flamingo and launched into a full account of the game, feeling immensely relieved to have someone to listen to her. The Cat apparently decided that having just a head in view was plenty, and no more of its body appeared.

"I don't think they play fair at all," Alice complained, "and they all argue so terribly that you can't even hear yourself think! They don't seem to have any actual rules—or if they do, nobody pays any attention to them! And you have no idea how confusing it is when all the equipment is alive. For instance, the hoop I'm supposed to shoot through next is currently strolling around on the other side of the court! And I would have hit the Queen's hedgehog just now, except it ran away the moment it saw mine rolling toward it!"

"How do you like the Queen?" the Cat asked in a low voice.

"Not at all," said Alice. "She's so completely—"

Just then, Alice realized that the Queen was standing right behind her, eavesdropping. She smoothly continued: "—likely to win that it's hardly worth finishing the match."

The Queen smiled and walked on.

"Who *are* you talking to?" asked the King, strolling up to Alice and staring at the Cat's head with great curiosity.

"It's a friend of mine—a Cheshire Cat," said Alice. "Allow me to introduce him."

"I don't like the look of him at all," said the King. "Still, he may kiss my hand if he likes."

"I'd really rather not," the Cat remarked.

"Don't be rude," said the King, "and don't look at me like that!" He shrank behind Alice as he spoke.

"A cat may look at a king," Alice said. "I read that in a book somewhere, though I don't remember where."

"Well, he must be removed," said the King very decidedly. Spotting the Queen passing by at that moment, he called out, "My dear! I want this cat removed at once!"

The Queen had only one way of resolving any difficulty, large or small. "Off with his head!" she shouted, without even looking around.

"I'll fetch the executioner myself!" said the King eagerly, and he scurried away.

Alice thought she might as well head back to see how the game was going, especially as she could hear the Queen's voice screaming furiously in the distance. She had already heard her sentence three players to death for missing their turns, and Alice didn't like the looks of things at all—the game was in such total chaos that she never knew whether it was her turn or not. So she went in search of her hedgehog.

Her hedgehog was currently locked in a fight with another hedgehog, which struck Alice as an excellent chance to croquet one ball into the other. The only problem was that her flamingo had wandered over to the other side of the garden, where Alice could see it flapping around helplessly, trying to fly up into a tree.

By the time she caught the flamingo and carried it back, the fight was over and both hedgehogs had vanished.

"Oh well, it doesn't matter much," thought Alice, "since all the hoops have walked away from this side of the lawn anyway."

She tucked the flamingo securely under her arm so it wouldn't escape again, and walked back to have a little more conversation with her friend.

When she returned to the Cheshire Cat, she was surprised to find quite a large crowd gathered around him. A heated three-way argument was underway between the executioner, the King, and the Queen, all talking at once, while everyone else stood in tense, uncomfortable silence.

The moment Alice appeared, all three appealed to her to settle the dispute. They barrage her with their arguments, but since they all talked over each other, it was remarkably difficult to make out what any of them were saying.

The executioner's argument was that you couldn't cut off a head unless there was a body to cut it off from; he had never had to do such a thing before, and he wasn't about to start at his time of life.

The King's argument was that anything that had a head could be beheaded, and that everyone needed to stop talking nonsense.

The Queen's argument was that if someone didn't resolve the matter immediately, she would have every single person executed on the spot. (It was this last remark that had made the whole crowd look so solemn and terrified.)

Alice could think of nothing else to say except, "He belongs to the Duchess. You'd better ask *her* about it."

"She's in prison," the Queen snapped at the executioner. "Fetch her here!"

The executioner took off like an arrow.

The moment he was gone, the Cat's head began to fade away. By the time the executioner returned with the Duchess, the Cat had disappeared entirely. The King and the executioner ran wildly up and down searching for it, while the rest of the party casually went back to the game.

# Chapter 9: The Mock Turtle's Story

"You have no idea how thrilled I am to see you again, you darling thing!" said the Duchess, slipping her arm affectionately through Alice's as they strolled off together.

Alice was relieved to find her in such a pleasant mood, thinking to herself that maybe it had just been the pepper that made her so furious back when they first met in the kitchen.

"When *I'm* a Duchess," she told herself—though not sounding especially hopeful about it—"I won't allow a single pinch of pepper in my kitchen. Soup does just fine without it. Maybe it's always pepper that makes people hot-tempered," she continued, quite pleased with herself for discovering a brand-new rule of nature, "and vinegar that makes them sour, and chamomile that makes them bitter, and candy and sweet treats that make children sweet-tempered. I only wish grown-ups knew *that*—then they wouldn't be so stingy with sweets, you know—"

By this point she had completely forgotten the Duchess was right there, so she jumped a little when a voice hissed right by her ear. "You're lost in thought, my dear, and that's making you forget to talk. I can't tell you right this second what the moral of that is, but it'll come to me in a moment."

"Maybe it doesn't have one," Alice ventured.

"Nonsense, child!" said the Duchess. "Everything has a moral, if only you're clever enough to find it." She squeezed even closer to Alice's side as she spoke.

Alice didn't really enjoy being squished so close to her. First, the Duchess was *strikingly* ugly. Second, she was the exact height to rest her chin squarely on Alice's shoulder—and it was an uncomfortably sharp chin. Still, Alice hated to be rude, so she put up with it as best she could.

"The game seems to be going a bit better now," Alice remarked, just to keep the conversation going.

"Indeed it is," said the Duchess. "And the moral of that is: 'Love makes the world go round!'"

"Someone told me," Alice whispered, "that it actually goes round by everybody minding their own business!"

"Oh well! It amounts to practically the same thing," said the Duchess, digging her sharp little chin into Alice's shoulder as she added, "and the moral of *that* is: 'Take care of the sense, and the sounds will take care of themselves.'"

"She certainly loves finding morals in everything!" Alice thought to herself.

"I bet you're wondering why I don't put my arm around your waist," the Duchess said after a pause. "The truth is, I'm not so sure about your flamingo's temper. Shall I run an experiment and find out?"

"It might bite," Alice replied cautiously, having zero desire to see the experiment carried out.

"Very true," said the Duchess. "Flamingos and mustard both have quite a bite. And the moral of that is: 'Birds of a feather flock together.'"

"Except mustard isn't a bird," Alice pointed out.

"Spot-on, as usual," said the Duchess. "You have such a clear way of putting things!"

"It's a mineral, I *think*," said Alice.

"Of course it is," agreed the Duchess, seemingly ready to agree with anything Alice said. "There's a massive mustard mine near here. And the moral of that is: 'The more there is that's mine, the less there is of yours.'"

"Oh, wait, I know!" exclaimed Alice, who hadn't paid any attention to that last comment. "It's a vegetable! It doesn't look like one, but it is."

"I completely agree," said the Duchess. "And the moral of that is: 'Be what you seem to be'—or, if you'd like it phrased more simply: 'Never imagine yourself not to be otherwise than what it might appear to others that what you were or might have been was not otherwise than what you had been would have appeared to them to be otherwise.'"

"I think I might understand that better if I saw it written down," Alice said politely. "I can't quite follow it out loud."

"That's nothing compared to what I could say if I really wanted to," the Duchess replied, sounding quite proud of herself.

"Please don't trouble yourself to make it any longer than that," said Alice.

"Oh, don't mention trouble!" the Duchess beamed. "Consider everything I've said so far a gift to you."

*What a cheap sort of gift,* Alice thought. *I'm certainly glad people don't give birthday presents like that!* But she didn't dare say it out loud.

"Thinking again?" the Duchess asked, giving Alice's shoulder another jab with her sharp little chin.

"I have every right to think," Alice snapped, starting to feel a bit annoyed.

"Just about as much right," said the Duchess, "as pigs have to fly. And the m—"

Right then, to Alice's utter astonishment, the Duchess's voice trailed off—cut short right in the middle of her favorite word, "moral"—and the arm linked through Alice's began to shake. Alice looked up, and there stood the Queen right in front of them, arms crossed, glaring like a brewing thunderstorm.

"L-lovely day, Your Majesty!" the Duchess squeaked in a faint, trembling voice.

"Consider yourself warned!" roared the Queen, stomping her foot on the ground. "Either you get lost, or your head comes off—and right this instant! Pick one!"

The Duchess picked her option instantly and vanished in a flash.

"Back to the game," the Queen commanded Alice. Alice was far too terrified to say a single word, so she meekly trailed behind her back to the croquet court.

The other guests had taken full advantage of the Queen's absence to lounge in the shade. The second they caught sight of her, though, they scrambled frantically back to the game, with the Queen remarking only that a single second's delay would cost them their heads.

The entire time they played, the Queen never stopped picking fights with the other players, constantly screaming, "Off with his head!" or "Off with her head!" Whenever someone received a death sentence, the card-soldiers dragged them away into custody. Of course, the soldiers had to stop bending over into croquet arches to do this, so after about thirty minutes, there wasn't a single arch left on the field. Every player except the King, the Queen, and Alice was under arrest and awaiting execution.

Finally the Queen stopped, completely out of breath, and turned to Alice. "Have you seen the Mock Turtle yet?"

"No," said Alice. "I don't even know what a Mock Turtle is."

"It's the creature they make Mock Turtle Soup from," said the Queen.

"I've never seen one or even heard of one," Alice said.

"Come along then," said the Queen, "and he'll tell you his life story."

As they walked away together, Alice overheard the King whisper to the gathered crowd, "You are all pardoned."

*Well, thank goodness for that!* she thought, having felt genuinely miserable about the sheer number of executions the Queen kept handing out.

Before long, they stumbled upon a Gryphon, fast asleep in the sun. (If you don't know what a Gryphon is, picture a mythical beast with the head and wings of an eagle and the body of a lion.)

"Up, you lazy creature!" barked the Queen. "Take this young lady to see the Mock Turtle so she can hear his story. I have to get back and oversee some executions I ordered."

She marched away, leaving Alice all alone with the Gryphon. Alice wasn't entirely thrilled by the look of the beast, but all things considered, she figured staying with it was a lot safer than following that bloodthirsty Queen. So she waited.

The Gryphon sat up, rubbed its eyes, and watched the Queen until she was completely out of sight. Then it chuckled. "What a riot!" muttered the Gryphon, half to itself and half to Alice.

"What's so funny?" Alice asked.

"Why, *her*!" said the Gryphon. "It's all in her head, you see. They never actually execute anyone! Come on!"

*Everyone around here just says 'Come on!'* Alice grumbled to herself as she trailed behind. *I've never been bossed around so much in my entire life!*

They hadn't walked far when they spotted the Mock Turtle perched sadly and alone on a little rocky ledge in the distance. As they drew closer, Alice could hear him heaving sighs so deep it sounded as if his heart were breaking into pieces. She felt terribly sorry for him.

"What's he so sad about?" she asked the Gryphon.

The Gryphon answered in practically the exact same words as before: "It's all in his head, that. He doesn't have anything to be sad about! Come on!"

They walked right up to the Mock Turtle. He looked at them with huge, teary eyes, but didn't say a word.

"This young lady here," said the Gryphon, "wants to hear your life story, she does."

"I'll tell it to her," the Mock Turtle announced in a deep, hollow voice. "Sit down, both of you, and don't utter a single peep until I'm finished."

So they sat down, and nobody spoke for several minutes.

Alice thought to herself, *I don't see how he can ever finish if he never even starts.* But she waited patiently.

"Once," the Mock Turtle said at last with a heavy sigh, "I was a real turtle."

These words were followed by an agonizingly long silence, broken only by an occasional grunt of "Hjckrrh!" from the Gryphon and the Mock Turtle's relentless, gut-wrenching sobs. Alice was on the verge of standing up and saying, "Thank you, sir, for your fascinating story," but she couldn't help thinking there *had* to be more to it. So she sat quietly and said nothing.

"Back when we were little," the Mock Turtle resumed at last, sounding a bit calmer though still sniffling now and then, "we went to school under the sea. Our headmaster was an old sea turtle—we used to call him Tortoise—"

"Why did you call him Tortoise if he wasn't one?" Alice asked.

"We called him Tortoise because he *taught us*!" snapped the Mock Turtle. "Honestly, you're awfully slow!"

"You should be ashamed of yourself, asking such a silly question," the Gryphon chimed in.

Then both creatures sat in dead silence, staring down at poor Alice until she wished the ground would open up and swallow her whole. Finally, the Gryphon nudged the Mock Turtle. "Move it along, old pal! Don't take all day!"

And the Mock Turtle continued:

"Yes, we went to school under the sea, whether you believe it or not—"

"I never said I didn't!" Alice cut in.

"You did," said the Mock Turtle.

"Quiet!" warned the Gryphon before Alice could get another word in.

The Mock Turtle went on.

"We had the finest education imaginable—we went to school every single day—"

"*I've* gone to day school too," said Alice. "You don't need to brag so much about that."

"With extras?" asked the Mock Turtle, looking a bit nervous.

"Yes," Alice said. "We learned French and music."

"And laundry?" asked the Mock Turtle.

"Of course not!" Alice scoffed.

"Ah! Then yours wasn't a truly top-tier school," said the Mock Turtle with enormous relief. "At *our* school, the bottom of the bill read: 'French, Music, and *Laundry*—extra.'"

"You couldn't have needed that very much," Alice pointed out, "living at the bottom of the sea."

"I couldn't afford to take it," the Mock Turtle sighed. "I only took the standard curriculum."

"What was that?" Alice inquired.

"Reeling and Writhing, of course, to start with," the Mock Turtle replied. "And after that, the different branches of Arithmetic: Ambition, Distraction, Uglification, and Derision."

"I've never heard of 'Uglification,'" Alice ventured to say. "What is it?"

The Gryphon threw up both paws in disbelief. "What! Never heard of uglifying?" it exclaimed. "You know what it means to beautify something, don't you?"

"Yes," Alice hesitated. "It means... to make something... prettier."

"Well, then," the Gryphon snapped, "if you don't know what it means to uglify, you really *are* an idiot."

Alice didn't feel like asking any more questions on that topic, so she turned back to the Mock Turtle. "What else did you have to learn?"

"Well, there was Mystery," the Mock Turtle counted off the subjects on his flippers, "Mystery, ancient and modern, alongside Seaography. Then came Drawling—the Drawling master was an old conger eel who came once a week. *He* taught us Drawling, Stretching, and Fainting in Coils."

"What was *that* like?" asked Alice.

"Well, I can't demonstrate it for you myself," the Mock Turtle said. "I'm far too stiff. And the Gryphon never took it."

"Never had the time," said the Gryphon. "I studied under the Classics master instead. An old crab, that one."

"I never took his class," the Mock Turtle sighed. "He taught Laughing and Grief, or so they told us."

"He did indeed, he did indeed," the Gryphon agreed, sighing in turn. Both creatures buried their faces in their paws.

"And how many hours a day did you have classes?" Alice asked, eager to steer the conversation elsewhere.

"Ten hours the first day," said the Mock Turtle, "nine hours the next, and so on."

"What a bizarre schedule!" Alice exclaimed.

"That's why they're called lessons," remarked the Gryphon, "because they *lessen* from day to day."

This was an entirely new concept to Alice. She turned it over in her mind before asking her next question: "Then the eleventh day must have been a day off?"

"Naturally," said the Mock Turtle.

"And what did you do on the twelfth day?" Alice pressed eagerly.

"That's quite enough about lessons," the Gryphon cut in firmly. "Tell her about the games now."

# Chapter 10: The Lobster Quadrille

The Mock Turtle sighed heavily and wiped the back of one flipper across his eyes. He looked at Alice and tried to speak, but for a minute or two, choking sobs caught in his throat.

"Sounds like he's choked on a bone," the Gryphon grunted, setting to work shaking him and thumping him on the back.

At last, the Mock Turtle got his voice back. With tears streaming down his cheeks, he continued:

"You might not have spent much time living under the sea—"

("I haven't," Alice chimed in.)

"—and maybe you've never even been formally introduced to a lobster—"

(Alice started to say, "I once tasted—" but caught herself just in time and said, "No, never.")

"—so you couldn't possibly imagine what a marvelous thing a Lobster Quadrille is!"

"I really can't," Alice admitted. "What kind of dance is it?"

"Well," the Gryphon explained, "first you line up along the seashore—"

"*Two* lines!" the Mock Turtle cried. "Seals, turtles, salmon, and all the rest. Then, once you've cleared all the jellyfish out of the way—"

"*That* usually takes a while," the Gryphon interjected.

"—you step forward twice—"

"Each partner paired with a lobster!" shouted the Gryphon.

"Naturally," the Mock Turtle said. "Step forward twice, bow to your partners—"

"—switch lobsters, and step back in the same order," the Gryphon chimed in.

"Then, you see," the Mock Turtle continued, "you chuck the—"

"The lobsters!" the Gryphon hollered, leaping into the air.

"—as far out to sea as you possibly can—"

"Swim after them!" shrieked the Gryphon.

"Do a somersault in the waves!" shouted the Mock Turtle, dancing around frantically.

"Swap lobsters again!" bellowed the Gryphon at the top of its lungs.

"Head back to shore, and that's the end of the first routine," the Mock Turtle concluded, his voice suddenly dropping to a whisper.

The two creatures, who had been bouncing around like wild maniacs, abruptly sat back down, mournful and dead silent, staring at Alice.

"It sounds like a very lovely dance," Alice ventured timidly.

"Would you like to see a bit of it?" asked the Mock Turtle.

"I would love that," Alice replied.

"Come on, let's show her the first routine!" the Mock Turtle said to the Gryphon. "We can manage without lobsters. Who's going to sing?"

"Oh, *you* sing," said the Gryphon. "I've forgotten the words."

So they began dancing solemnly in circles around Alice, stomping on her toes every now and then whenever they passed too close, and waving their front paws to keep time while the Mock Turtle sang this, very slowly and sorrowfully:

> "Won't you walk a little faster?" said a whiting to a snail,  
> "There's a porpoise right behind us, and he's stepping on my tail!  
> Look how eagerly the lobsters and the turtles all advance—  
> They are waiting on the pebbles: will you come and join the dance?  
> Will you, won't you, will you, won't you, will you join the dance?  
> Will you, won't you, will you, won't you, won't you join the dance?  
>  
> "You can really have no notion how delightful it will be  
> When they pick us up and toss us, with the lobsters, out to sea!"  
> But the snail replied, "Too far, too far!" and gave a sideways glance,  
> Saying thanks, but no, he really could not, would not join the dance.  
> Would not, could not, would not, could not, would not join the dance,  
> Would not, could not, would not, could not, could not join the dance!  
>  
> "What does it matter how far we go?" his scaly friend replied,  
> "There's another pleasant shoreline waiting on the other side.  
> The farther off from England, why, the closer into France!  
> So don't turn pale, beloved snail, but come and join the dance!  
> Will you, won't you, will you, won't you, will you join the dance?  
> Will you, won't you, will you, won't you, won't you join the dance?"

"Thank you, that was such a fascinating dance to watch," Alice said, secretly relieved that it had finally ended. "And I really enjoyed that bizarre song about the whiting!"

"Oh, speaking of whitings," said the Mock Turtle, "they—well, you've seen them, haven't you?"

"Yes," Alice replied, "I've often seen them at dinn—" She bit her tongue just in time.

"I haven't the foggiest where Dinn might be," said the Mock Turtle, "but if you've seen them so often, you surely know what they look like."

"I think so," Alice said thoughtfully. "They have their tails in their mouths—and they're coated all over in breadcrumbs."

"You're wrong about the crumbs," said the Mock Turtle. "Breadcrumbs would wash right off in the sea! But they *do* have their tails in their mouths, and the reason is—"

Here the Mock Turtle yawned and closed his eyes.

"—tell her the reason and all that," he mumbled to the Gryphon.

"The reason is," said the Gryphon, "that they *insisted* on joining the lobsters for the dance. So they got tossed out to sea. So they fell a terribly long way. So their tails got stuck fast in their mouths. So they could never pry them back out. End of story."

"Thank you," said Alice. "That's really interesting. I never knew so much about whitings before."

"I can tell you even more than that if you want," said the Gryphon. "Do you know why it's called a whiting?"

"I never really thought about it," said Alice. "Why?"

"*It cleans the boots and shoes*," the Gryphon answered with utter solemnity.

Alice was completely baffled. "Cleans boots and shoes?" she repeated in bewilderment.

"Well, what do you shine *your* shoes with?" asked the Gryphon. "I mean, what makes them so shiny?"

Alice looked down at her shoes and paused to think before answering. "They're polished with blacking, I believe."

"Boots and shoes under the sea," the Gryphon said in a low, know-it-all voice, "are polished with whiting. Now you know."

"And what are they made of?" Alice asked, genuinely curious.

"Soles and eels, obviously!" the Gryphon snapped, losing patience. "Any shrimp could have told you that!"

"If I'd been the whiting," Alice remarked, her mind still wandering back to the song, "I would have told that porpoise, 'Back off, please—we don't want *you* tagging along!'"

"They had no choice but to bring him along," said the Mock Turtle. "No sensible fish would ever travel anywhere without a porpoise."

"Really? Wouldn't it?" Alice asked in surprise.

"Of course not!" said the Mock Turtle. "Why, if a fish came up to *me* and said he was going on a trip, I'd say, 'For what porpoise?'"

"Don't you mean 'purpose'?" asked Alice.

"I say what I mean," the Mock Turtle huffed, sounding quite offended.

The Gryphon jumped in: "Come on, let's hear some of *your* adventures now."

"I could tell you about my adventures, starting from this morning," Alice offered a little tentatively. "There's no point in going back to yesterday, though, because I was an entirely different person back then."

"Explain that!" said the Mock Turtle.

"No, no! Adventures first!" the Gryphon insisted impatiently. "Explanations take such an exhausting amount of time."

So Alice started recounting her adventures, beginning from the moment she first spotted the White Rabbit. She felt quite nervous at first, as both creatures crowded in uncomfortably close—one on each side—staring with their eyes and mouths hanging wide open. But she gathered confidence as she kept talking. Her audience stayed totally silent until she reached the part where she recited "You are old, Father William" to the Caterpillar and the words came out completely scrambled. At that, the Mock Turtle drew a deep breath and murmured, "Well, that is very strange."

"As weird as it gets," agreed the Gryphon.

"The words all came out wrong!" the Mock Turtle repeated thoughtfully. "I'd love to hear her try reciting something right now. Tell her to start." He looked at the Gryphon as though assuming it had official authority over Alice.

"Stand up and recite ''Tis the voice of the sluggard,'" ordered the Gryphon.

*The way these animals boss people around and force them to recite school lessons!* Alice thought. *I might as well be stuck in a classroom.*

Still, she got to her feet and began to recite. But her mind was so swirling with the Lobster Quadrille that she barely knew what was coming out of her mouth, and the words turned out very bizarre indeed:

> "'Tis the voice of the Lobster; I heard him declare:  
> 'You've baked me too brown, I must sugar my hair.'  
> Just as ducks use their eyelids, so he with his nose  
> Adjusts his belt buckles and turns out his toes.  
> When the beaches are dry, he's as bold as a lark,  
> Talking trash with a sneer of his neighbor the Shark;  
> But as soon as the tide rolls and sharks swim around,  
> His bold voice turns shaky—a terrified sound."

"That's definitely different from what *I* used to recite as a kid," remarked the Gryphon.

"Well, I've never heard it before," said the Mock Turtle, "but it sounds like extraordinary nonsense."

Alice said nothing. She slumped back down with her face buried in her hands, wondering if anything would *ever* make normal sense again.

"I'd like to have that explained," said the Mock Turtle.

"She can't explain it," the Gryphon said quickly. "Move on to the next verse."

"But what about his toes?" the Mock Turtle pressed on. "How on earth *could* he turn them out with his nose, you know?"

"It's the first position in ballet," Alice offered, but she was completely bewildered by the whole thing and desperate to change the subject.

"Get on with the next verse," the Gryphon urged impatiently. "It starts: 'I passed by his garden.'"

Alice didn't dare disobey, even though she was certain it would turn out all wrong. In a shaky voice, she continued:

> "I walked by his garden and saw with one eye  
> How the Owl and the Panther were sharing a pie:  
> The Panther took pie crust, and gravy, and meat,  
> While the Owl had the dish as his share of the treat.  
> When the pie was all finished, the Owl, as a boon,  
> Was kindly permitted to pocket the spoon,  
> While the Panther claimed knife and fork back with a growl,  
> And concluded the banquet—"

"What is the point of reciting all that stuff," the Mock Turtle cut in, "if you don't explain it as you go along? It's easily the most confusing thing *I've* ever heard!"

"Yeah, you'd better stop there," the Gryphon agreed, and Alice was only too happy to quit.

"Shall we try another routine of the Lobster Quadrille?" the Gryphon asked. "Or would you prefer the Mock Turtle to sing you a song?"

"Oh, a song, please, if the Mock Turtle wouldn't mind!" Alice answered, sounding so enthusiastic that the Gryphon grumbled in a slightly offended tone: "Hmph! There's no accounting for taste! Sing her 'Turtle Soup,' will you, old boy?"

The Mock Turtle let out a heavy sigh and, in a voice that broke with sobs every few seconds, began to sing:

> "Beautiful Soup, so rich and green,  
> Piping hot in a steaming tureen!  
> Who wouldn't bow for a savory scoop?  
> Soup of the evening, beautiful Soup!  
> Soup of the evening, beautiful Soup!  
> &nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Beau—ootiful Soo—oop!  
> &nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Beau—ootiful Soo—oop!  
> Soo—oop of the e—e—evening,  
> &nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Beautiful, beautiful Soup!  
>  
> "Beautiful Soup! Who cares for fish,  
> Roasted poultry, or any other dish?  
> Who wouldn't trade all they have for a scoop,  
> For pennies' worth only of beautiful Soup?  
> Pennies' worth only of beautiful Soup?  
> &nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Beau—ootiful Soo—oop!  
> &nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Beau—ootiful Soo—oop!  
> Soo—oop of the e—e—evening,  
> &nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Beautiful, beauti—FUL SOUP!"

"Chorus again!" cried the Gryphon. The Mock Turtle had only just started to repeat it when a shout echoed in the distance: "The trial is starting!"

"Come on!" shouted the Gryphon. Grabbing Alice by the hand, it dashed off without waiting for the end of the song.

"What trial is it?" Alice panted as she ran.

The Gryphon only yelled, "Come on!" and sprinted even faster, while drifting fainter and fainter on the breeze behind them came those mournful words:

> "Soo—oop of the e—e—evening,  
> &nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Beautiful, beautiful Soup!"

# Chapter 11: Who Stole the Tarts?

When they arrived, the King and Queen of Hearts were seated on their thrones, surrounded by an enormous crowd—all sorts of small birds and animals, as well as the entire deck of cards. The Knave stood before them in chains, guarded by a soldier on either side. Near the King stood the White Rabbit, holding a trumpet in one hand and a parchment scroll in the other. Right in the middle of the courtroom sat a table bearing a large platter of tarts. They looked so delicious that just staring at them made Alice's mouth water.

"I really wish they'd hurry up and finish the trial," she thought, "and start passing around the refreshments!" But there didn't seem to be any chance of that happening anytime soon, so she began looking around at everything to pass the time.

Alice had never set foot in a court of law before, but she had read about them in books, and she was delighted to find that she recognized almost everything there.

"That's the judge," she told herself, "because of his big powdered wig."

The judge, as it turned out, was the King. And since he was wearing his royal crown perched directly over the wig (if you're wondering how he managed that, picture a heavy gold crown balanced precariously on a mountain of curls), he didn't look comfortable in the least—and it certainly wasn't a flattering look.

"And that's the jury box," thought Alice, "and those twelve creatures"—she had to say "creatures," you see, because some were animals and some were birds—"I suppose they're the jurors." She repeated the word to herself two or three times, feeling quite proud of knowing it. She figured—and rightly so—that very few girls her age knew what a juror even was. (Though calling them "jurymen" would have worked just as well.)

The twelve jurors were all writing away furiously on chalk slates.

"What are they doing?" Alice whispered to the Gryphon. "They can't possibly have anything to write down yet—the trial hasn't even started!"

"They're writing down their own names," the Gryphon whispered back, "in case they forget them before the trial is over."

"How ridiculous!" Alice blurted out in a loud, indignant voice. But she stopped herself abruptly, because the White Rabbit shouted, "Silence in the court!" and the King adjusted his spectacles and peered anxiously around the room to see who had spoken.

Alice could see, just as clearly as if she were peering right over their shoulders, that every single juror was scribbling "How ridiculous!" onto their slates. She could even tell that one of them didn't know how to spell "ridiculous" and had to nudge his neighbor to ask.

"What an absolute mess their slates will be in before this trial is over!" thought Alice.

One of the jurors had a pencil that squeaked horribly. Naturally, Alice could *not* stand the sound. She crept around the courtroom, slipped up right behind him, and quickly found an opportunity to snatch it away. She was so quick that the poor little juror—who happened to be Bill the Lizard—had no idea what had happened to it. After hunting everywhere in confusion, he was forced to write with one finger for the rest of the day. That wasn't much help, of course, since a finger leaves no mark on a slate.

"Herald, read the charges!" commanded the King.

At this, the White Rabbit blew three sharp blasts on his trumpet, unrolled the parchment scroll, and read aloud:

> "The Queen of Hearts, she baked some tarts,  
> All on a summer day;  
> The Knave of Hearts, he stole those tarts,  
> And took them clean away!"

"Consider your verdict," the King instructed the jury.

"Not yet, not yet!" the White Rabbit interrupted in a hurry. "There's an awful lot to do before we get to that!"

"Call the first witness," said the King.

The White Rabbit blew three blasts on the trumpet and called out, "First witness!"

The first witness was the Hatter. He hurried in holding a teacup in one hand and a slice of buttered bread in the other.

"I beg your pardon, Your Majesty," he began, "for bringing these in, but I hadn't quite finished my tea when I was summoned."

"You should have finished," said the King. "When did you start?"

The Hatter glanced at the March Hare, who had followed him into the courtroom arm-in-arm with the Dormouse.

"The fourteenth of March, I *think* it was," he said.

"The fifteenth," corrected the March Hare.

"The sixteenth," added the Dormouse.

"Write that down," the King told the jury.

The jurors eagerly jotted down all three dates on their slates, added them together, and then converted the total into pounds and pence.

"Take off your hat," the King ordered the Hatter.

"It isn't mine," said the Hatter.

"*Stolen!*" gasped the King, turning to the jury, who instantly made a note of the theft.

"I keep them to sell," the Hatter explained. "None of them belong to me. I'm a hatter, you see."

At that, the Queen put on her spectacles and began glaring intently at the Hatter, who turned pale and started fidgeting.

"Give your testimony," said the King, "and don't be nervous, or I'll have you executed on the spot."

This didn't encourage the witness in the slightest. He kept shifting from one foot to the other, glancing nervously at the Queen. In his utter panic, he took a huge bite right out of his teacup instead of his bread and butter.

Right at that moment, Alice felt a very strange sensation. It puzzled her for several seconds until she realized what was happening: she was beginning to grow larger again! At first, she thought about getting up and walking out of the courtroom, but on second thought, she decided to stay right where she was as long as there was still room for her.

"I wish you wouldn't crowd me so much," said the Dormouse, who was sitting right beside her. "I can barely breathe."

"I can't help it," Alice said meekly. "I'm growing."

"You've got no right to grow *here*," said the Dormouse.

"Don't be ridiculous," Alice replied more boldly. "You know perfectly well you're growing too."

"Yes, but *I* grow at a reasonable pace," said the Dormouse, "not in that absurd, runaway fashion!" He got up in a huff and stomped across to the other side of the courtroom.

The entire time, the Queen hadn't taken her eyes off the Hatter. Just as the Dormouse crossed the room, she told one of the court officers, "Bring me the list of singers from the last concert!"

Hearing this, the wretched Hatter trembled so hard that both of his shoes shook right off his feet.

"Give your testimony!" the King barked angrily. "Or I'll have you executed whether you're nervous or not!"

"I'm a poor man, Your Majesty," the Hatter began in a shaking voice, "—and I hadn't begun my tea—not for more than a week or so—and what with the bread and butter getting so thin—and the twinkling of the tea—"

"The twinkling of the *what?*" asked the King.

"It *began* with the tea," the Hatter stammered.

"Of course twinkling begins with a T!" snapped the King. "Do you take me for an idiot? Keep going!"

"I'm a poor man," the Hatter continued, "and pretty much everything twinkled after that—except the March Hare said—"

"I didn't!" the March Hare interrupted in a frantic hurry.

"You did!" said the Hatter.

"I deny it completely!" insisted the March Hare.

"He denies it," said the King. "Strike that part from the record."

"Well, at any rate, the Dormouse said—" the Hatter went on, glancing around anxiously to see if he would deny it too. But the Dormouse didn't deny a thing, being fast asleep.

"After that," continued the Hatter, "I sliced some more bread and butter—"

"But what did the Dormouse say?" asked one of the jurors.

"That part I can't remember," confessed the Hatter.

"You *must* remember," the King warned him, "or I'll have you executed."

The miserable Hatter dropped his teacup and bread and butter, sinking down onto one knee.

"I'm a poor man, Your Majesty," he whimpered.

"You're a *very* poor *speaker*," remarked the King.

At this, one of the guinea pigs cheered—and was instantly suppressed by the courtroom bailiffs. (Since "suppressed" is a rather formal legal word, let me explain how they did it: they had a large canvas sack with drawstrings at the opening. They slipped the guinea pig inside headfirst, pulled the strings tight, and sat on it.)

"I'm glad I finally saw how that's done," thought Alice. "I've so often read in the papers at the end of trials, 'There was a brief attempt at applause, which was immediately suppressed by the officers of the court,' and I never quite understood what it meant until now!"

"If that's all you know about the matter, you may step down," the King continued.

"I can't get any lower," said the Hatter. "I'm already kneeling on the floor."

"Then you may *sit* down," the King replied.

At that, the other guinea pig cheered—and was promptly suppressed too.

"Well, that takes care of the guinea pigs!" thought Alice. "Now things should run much smoother."

"I'd really rather finish my tea," said the Hatter, eyeing the Queen nervously as she scanned the list of singers.

"You may go," said the King.

The Hatter bolted out of the courtroom so fast he didn't even wait to put his shoes back on.

"—And just take his head off outside!" the Queen shouted after him to one of the guards. But the Hatter was already long gone before the guard could even reach the door.

"Call the next witness!" said the King.

The next witness was the Duchess's cook. She marched in carrying a massive pepper shaker, and Alice guessed who it was before she even stepped inside, thanks to everyone near the doorway sneezing all at once.

"Give your testimony," said the King.

"Nope," said the cook.

The King looked nervously at the White Rabbit, who whispered in a low voice, "Your Majesty will have to cross-examine *this* witness."

"Well, if I must, I suppose I must," the King sighed with a gloomy air. Folding his arms and squinting fiercely at the cook until his eyes were practically shut, he asked in a deep voice, "What are tarts made of?"

"Pepper, mostly," said the cook.

"Molasses," mumbled a sleepy voice behind her.

"Grab that Dormouse!" shrieked the Queen. "Behead that Dormouse! Throw that Dormouse out of court! Suppress him! Pinch him! Cut off his whiskers!"

For several chaotic minutes, the entire courtroom was in total pandemonium while everyone scrambled to throw the Dormouse out. By the time everyone settled back down, the cook had vanished completely.

"Oh, never mind!" said the King, looking enormously relieved. "Call the next witness." Then he leaned over and whispered to the Queen, "Really, my dear, *you* must cross-examine the next one. It's giving me a splitting headache!"

Alice watched the White Rabbit fumble through the witness list, eager to see who could possibly be called next. "They haven't gathered much evidence *yet*," she thought to herself.

Imagine her utter shock when the White Rabbit called out at the top of his shrill little voice:

"Alice!"

# Chapter 12: Alice's Evidence

"Here!" cried Alice.

In the excitement of the moment, she completely forgot just how enormous she had grown over the last few minutes. She leaped to her feet in such a rush that the hem of her skirt caught the jury box, flipping it completely upside down. All twelve jurors tumbled head over heels onto the crowd below, sprawling everywhere and flapping about helplessly. The sight reminded Alice vividly of a bowl of goldfish she had accidentally knocked over the week before.

"Oh, I am *so* sorry!" she gasped in utter dismay. She immediately began scooping them up as fast as she could. The memory of the spilled goldfish kept racing through her mind, giving her the frantic, illogical impression that if the jurors weren't gathered up and put back in their box immediately, they would die.

"The trial cannot proceed," the King declared in a solemn, heavy voice, "until every single juror is back in their proper place—*every last one*." He stared hard at Alice as he spoke, emphasizing the point.

Alice looked down into the jury box and noticed that, in her haste, she had jammed Bill the Lizard in upside down. The poor little fellow was waving his tail pathetically in the air, completely unable to budge. She quickly lifted him out and turned him right-side up.

"Not that it makes much difference," she thought to herself. "I imagine he'll be *just* as useful to the trial one way up as the other."

As soon as the jury had recovered from the shock of being tipped over, and their slates and pencils had been rounded up and handed back, they went diligently to work writing out an official history of the accident. All except Bill the Lizard, that is—he seemed far too overwhelmed to do anything except sit with his mouth hanging open, staring blankly up at the courtroom ceiling.

"What do you know about this business?" the King asked Alice.

"Nothing," said Alice.

"Nothing *whatever?*" persisted the King.

"Nothing whatever," said Alice.

"That's very important," the King told the jury.

The jurors were just beginning to write this down on their slates when the White Rabbit interrupted.

"*Un*important, Your Majesty means, of course," the Rabbit said in a very respectful tone, though he was frowning and making frantic faces at the King as he spoke.

"*Un*important, of course! That's what I meant," the King hastily corrected himself. Then he went on muttering under his breath, "Important... unimportant... unimportant... important..." as if trying to see which word sounded best.

Some of the jurors wrote down "important," while others wrote "unimportant." Alice could see this clearly because she was close enough to look down over their slates. "Not that it matters in the slightest," she thought.

Just then, the King, who had been scribbling away in his notebook for several minutes, barked out, "Silence!" He adjusted his glasses and read aloud from his book:

"Rule Forty-Two: *All persons more than a mile high must immediately leave the court.*"

Everyone turned to stare at Alice.

"*I'm* not a mile high," said Alice.

"You are," said the King.

"Nearly two miles high, in fact," the Queen chimed in.

"Well, I'm not leaving either way," said Alice. "Besides, that's not a real rule—you just made it up this very second!"

"It's the oldest rule in the book!" the King protested.

"Then it ought to be Rule Number One," said Alice.

The King turned pale and slammed his notebook shut in a hurry.

"Consider your verdict," he told the jury in a low, trembling voice.

"There's more evidence to come yet, if it please Your Majesty!" shouted the White Rabbit, jumping up in a great hurry. "This paper was just picked up off the floor."

"What's in it?" demanded the Queen.

"I haven't opened it yet," said the White Rabbit, "but it appears to be a letter written by the prisoner to... to somebody."

"It must be that," agreed the King, "unless it was written to nobody, which isn't very common, you know."

"Who is it addressed to?" asked one of the jurors.

"It isn't addressed to anyone," said the White Rabbit. "In fact, there's nothing written on the *outside* at all." Unfolding the paper as he spoke, he added, "Wait, it isn't a letter after all—it's a set of verses!"

"Is it in the prisoner's handwriting?" asked another juror.

"No, it isn't," said the White Rabbit, "and that's the strangest thing about it." (The jury all looked thoroughly puzzled.)

"He must have imitated somebody else's handwriting!" said the King. (At this, the jurors' faces all brightened up again.)

"Please, Your Majesty," pleaded the Knave, "I didn't write it, and they can't prove I did! There's no signature at the end."

"If you didn't sign it," said the King, "that only makes things worse! You *must* have meant some mischief, or else you would have signed your name like an honest man."

A general round of applause broke out at this. It was easily the cleverest thing the King had said all day.

"That *proves* his guilt!" said the Queen.

"It proves nothing of the sort!" Alice said firmly. "Why, you don't even know what the verses are about!"

"Read them," said the King.

The White Rabbit put on his spectacles. "Where should I begin, Your Majesty?" he asked.

"Begin at the beginning," the King said gravely, "and go on until you come to the end: then stop."

These were the verses the White Rabbit read:

> "They told me you had been to her,  
> And mentioned me to him:  
> She gave me a good character,  
> But said I could not swim.  
>  
> He sent them word I had not gone  
> (We know it to be true):  
> If she should push the matter on,  
> What would become of you?  
>  
> I gave her one, they gave him two,  
> You gave us three or more;  
> They all returned from him to you,  
> Though they were mine before.  
>  
> If I or she should chance to be  
> Involved in this affair,  
> He trusts to you to set them free,  
> Exactly as we were.  
>  
> My notion was that you had been  
> (Before she had this fit)  
> An obstacle that came between  
> Him, and ourselves, and it.  
>  
> Don’t let him know she liked them best,  
> For this must ever be  
> A secret, kept from all the rest,  
> Between yourself and me."

"That's the most important piece of evidence we've heard yet," said the King, rubbing his hands together. "So now let the jury—"

"If any one of them can explain it," said Alice—she had grown so huge in the last few minutes that she wasn't the least bit afraid of interrupting him—"I'll give them a dollar! *I* don't believe there's a single shred of meaning in it."

Every juror wrote down on their slates, "*She* doesn't believe there's a single shred of meaning in it," but not one of them attempted to explain the paper.

"If there's no meaning in it," said the King, "that saves a world of trouble, you know, since we don't have to try and find any. And yet... I don't know," he went on, spreading the paper out on his knee and squinting at it with one eye. "I seem to see some meaning in it after all. '*...Said I could not swim...*' You can't swim, can you?" he asked, turning to the Knave.

The Knave shook his head mournfully. "Do I look like I can?" he asked. (Which he certainly did *not*, being made entirely of cardboard.)

"Checks out so far," muttered the King, reading through the lines to himself: "'*We know it to be true*—' that's the jury, of course. '*I gave her one, they gave him two*—' why, that must be what he did with the tarts, you see—"

"Hold on, it goes on: '*They all returned from him to you,*'" said Alice.

"Why, there they are!" shouted the King triumphantly, pointing to the tarts on the table. "Nothing could be clearer than *that*! And then again—'*before she had this fit*—' You never had fits, my dear, did you?" he asked the Queen.

"Never!" roared the Queen furiously, throwing a heavy inkstand at the Lizard as she spoke. (Unfortunate little Bill had stopped writing on his slate with his finger since it left no mark, but he hurriedly began again, using the ink trickling down his face for as long as it lasted.)

"Then the words don't *fit* you," said the King, beaming around the courtroom with a proud smile.

Dead silence.

"It's a pun!" the King added in an offended tone.

At once, everybody burst out laughing.

"Let the jury consider their verdict," the King commanded, for about the twentieth time that day.

"No, no!" said the Queen. "Sentence first—verdict afterwards!"

"That's pure nonsense!" Alice said loudly. "The idea of having the sentence first!"

"Hold your tongue!" the Queen shrieked, turning purple.

"I won't!" said Alice.

"Off with her head!" the Queen shouted at the top of her voice.

Nobody moved.

"Who cares what you say?" said Alice. (She had grown back to her full normal size by this time.) "You're nothing but a pack of cards!"

At this, the entire deck rose up into the air and came flying down upon her in a swirling swarm. Alice gave a little scream—half in fright and half in anger—and tried to beat them off...

...Only to find herself lying on the grassy riverbank, her head in the lap of her sister, who was gently brushing away a few dry leaves that had fluttered down from the trees onto her face.

"Wake up, Alice dear!" said her sister. "Why, what a long sleep you've had!"

"Oh, I've had such a strange dream!" said Alice.

And right there by the river, she recounted to her sister, as well as she could remember them, all the bizarre adventures you've just been reading about. When she had finished, her sister kissed her forehead and said, "It *was* a strange dream, dear, certainly. But now run inside for your tea—it's getting late."

So Alice jumped up and ran off, thinking the whole way about what a wonderful dream it had been.

Her sister sat still just as Alice had left her, resting her chin on her hand, watching the setting sun, and thinking of little Alice and all her extraordinary adventures, until she too began dreaming in her own way. And this was her dream:

First, she dreamed of little Alice herself. Once again, those tiny hands were clasped gently upon her knee, and the bright, eager eyes were looking up into hers. She could hear the lively tones of Alice's voice and see that endearing little flick of her head to keep back the stray wisps of hair that *would* always get into her eyes. And still, as she listened—or seemed to listen—the whole world around her became alive with the wondrous creatures of her little sister's dream.

The tall grass rustled at her feet as the White Rabbit hurried by. The frightened Mouse splashed his way through the ripples of the nearby pool. She could hear the clatter of teacups as the March Hare and his friends shared their never-ending meal, and the piercing voice of the Queen shouting orders to execute her unfortunate guests. Once again, the baby-turned-pig was sneezing on the Duchess's knee while plates and platters crashed all around. Once more the shriek of the Gryphon, the squeaking of the Lizard's slate pencil, and the muffled gasps of the suppressed guinea pigs filled the air, mingled with the distant sobs of the miserable Mock Turtle.

So she sat on with closed eyes, half-believing herself in Wonderland, though she knew she had only to open them again and everything would snap back to ordinary reality. The grass would be only rustling in the breeze, and the pool rippling with the sway of the reeds. The rattling teacups would turn into the soft tinkling of sheep bells, and the Queen's shrill cries into the distant calls of a shepherd boy. The baby's sneezes, the Gryphon's shriek, and all the other strange noises would dissolve into the familiar bustle of the busy farmyard, while the lowing of cattle in the distance would take the place of the Mock Turtle's sorrowful sobs.

Lastly, she pictured how this same little sister of hers would, in the years to come, grow into a woman of her own. She knew Alice would keep, throughout all her grown-up years, the simple, loving heart of her childhood. She pictured Alice gathering other little children around her, making *their* eyes shine bright and eager with many a fantastical tale—perhaps even the dream of Wonderland from long ago. She would feel with all their simple sorrows, and find pure pleasure in all their simple joys, remembering her own childhood and the happy summer days.

**THE END**
