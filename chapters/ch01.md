# Chapter 1: Down the Rabbit-Hole

Alice was beginning to get thoroughly tired of sitting next to her sister on the grassy bank with nothing to do. Once or twice she had peeked into the book her sister was reading, but it didn't have any pictures or conversations in it. "And what is the point of a book," Alice thought, "without pictures or conversations?"

So she was turning over in her mind—as well as she could, since the hot afternoon made her feel awfully sleepy and sluggish—whether the pleasure of weaving a daisy chain would be worth the trouble of getting up and picking the daisies, when suddenly a White Rabbit with pink eyes dashed right past her.

There wasn't anything so *very* remarkable about that, nor did Alice think it so *very* strange to hear the Rabbit mutter to itself, "Oh dear! Oh dear! I'm going to be late!" (When she thought it over later, it occurred to her that she really ought to have wondered at this, but at the time it all seemed completely natural.) But when the Rabbit actually *pulled a pocket watch out of its vest pocket*, checked the time, and hurried on, Alice leaped to her feet. It flashed across her mind that she had never before seen a rabbit with a vest pocket, let alone a watch to take out of it! Burning with curiosity, she ran across the field after it, arriving just in time to see it pop down a large rabbit hole under the hedge.

A second later, down went Alice right after it, never once stopping to consider how on earth she was going to get out again.

The rabbit hole ran straight on like a tunnel for a little way, and then plunged suddenly downward—so suddenly that Alice didn't have a single second to think about stopping herself before she found herself falling down an extraordinarily deep well.

Either the well was unbelievably deep, or she was falling very slowly, because she had plenty of time on the way down to look around and wonder what was going to happen next. First, she tried to peer down and make out what was coming, but it was far too dark to see anything. Then she looked at the walls of the shaft and noticed they were lined with cupboards and bookshelves. Here and there, she saw maps and pictures hanging from pegs. As she floated past one of the shelves, she grabbed a jar. It was labeled **ORANGE MARMALADE**, but to her great disappointment, it was completely empty. She didn't want to drop the jar for fear of crushing someone below, so she managed to tuck it into one of the cupboards as she drifted past.

"Well!" Alice thought to herself. "After a fall like this, tumbling down the stairs will be a piece of cake! Everyone at home will think I'm so brave! Why, I wouldn't say a word about it even if I fell off the roof of the house!" (Which was almost certainly true.)

Down, down, down. Was the fall *never* going to come to an end? "I wonder how many miles I've fallen by now?" she said out loud. "I must be getting somewhere near the center of the Earth. Let me see: that would be about four thousand miles down, I think—" (Alice had learned a number of things like this during lessons in the schoolroom, and although this wasn't exactly a great opportunity to show off her knowledge since there was no one around to listen, it was still good practice to recite it) "—yes, that's about the right distance. But then, I wonder what Latitude or Longitude I've reached?" (Alice didn't have the foggiest idea what Latitude was, or Longitude either, but she thought they were wonderfully grand words to say.)

Before long, she started up again. "I wonder if I'll fall right *through* the Earth! How funny it'll be to pop out among the people who walk with their heads hanging down! The Antipathies, I think—" (she was rather glad nobody was listening this time, since that didn't sound quite like the right word) "—though I'll have to ask them what the country is called, you know. 'Excuse me, ma'am, is this New Zealand or Australia?'" (And she tried to curtsy as she spoke—just imagine curtsying while tumbling through the air! Do you think you could manage it?) "'And what an ignorant little girl she'll think I am for asking! No, it will never do to ask. Maybe I'll see the name written up somewhere.'"

Down, down, down. There was nothing else to do, so Alice soon started talking again. "Dinah will miss me terribly tonight, I'm sure!" (Dinah was the cat.) "I hope they remember her saucer of milk at tea time. Oh, Dinah dear! I wish you were down here with me! There aren't any mice in the air, I'm afraid, but you might catch a bat, and a bat is very much like a mouse, you know. But do cats eat bats, I wonder?" Here Alice began to feel rather sleepy, and she went on repeating to herself in a dreamy, hypnotic voice, "Do cats eat bats? Do cats eat bats?" and sometimes, "Do bats eat cats?" Because, you see, since she couldn't answer either question, it didn't really matter which way she put it. She felt herself nodding off and had just started dreaming that she was walking hand in hand with Dinah, asking her very seriously, "Now, Dinah, tell me the honest truth: have you ever eaten a bat?" when suddenly—*thump! thump!*—down she landed on a heap of dry sticks and leaves, and the fall was over.

Alice wasn't hurt in the slightest, and she leaped to her feet in an instant. She looked up, but overhead was pitch black. Before her stretched another long corridor, and the White Rabbit was still in sight, hurrying down it. There wasn't a second to lose! Off sprinted Alice like the wind, catching up just in time to hear him squeak as he rounded a corner: "Oh, my ears and whiskers, look how late it's getting!" She was right on his heels when she turned the corner, but the Rabbit had completely vanished. Instead, she found herself in a long, low-ceilinged hall illuminated by a row of lamps hanging from the roof.

There were doors all around the hall, but every single one of them was locked. After Alice had walked all the way down one side and back up the other, trying every doorknob, she walked dejectedly down the middle, wondering how she was ever going to get out again.

Suddenly, she came upon a little three-legged table made entirely of solid glass. There was nothing on it except a tiny golden key, and Alice's first thought was that it might belong to one of the doors in the hall. But alas! Either the locks were too large or the key was too small, but in any case, it wouldn't open a single one. Walking around the hall a second time, however, she noticed a low curtain she hadn't spotted before, and behind it was a little door about fifteen inches high. She tried the tiny golden key in the lock, and to her immense delight, it fitted!

Alice opened the door and found that it led into a small passage, hardly bigger than a rat hole. She knelt down and peered along the tunnel into the loveliest garden you ever saw. How she ached to get out of that gloomy hall and wander among those beds of bright flowers and cool, splashing fountains! But she couldn't even squeeze her head through the doorway. "And even if my head would fit," poor Alice thought, "it wouldn't be much use without my shoulders. Oh, how I wish I could collapse like a telescope! I think I could, if only I knew where to begin." You see, so many extraordinary things had happened lately that Alice had begun to think very few things were truly impossible.

There seemed to be no point in waiting by the little door, so she went back to the table, half hoping she might find another key, or at least an instruction book on how to fold people up like telescopes. This time, she found a little bottle on it ("Which definitely wasn't here before," Alice noted). Tied around the neck of the bottle was a paper tag, with the words **DRINK ME** beautifully printed in large letters.

It was all very well to say "Drink me," but sensible little Alice wasn't going to do *that* in a hurry. "No, I'll check first," she said, "and see whether it's marked *poison* or not." She had read several cautionary tales about children who had been burned, or eaten alive by wild beasts and other unpleasant things, all because they *refused* to remember the simple rules their friends had taught them—such as, a red-hot fire poker will burn you if you hold it too long; if you cut your finger *deeply* with a knife, it usually bleeds; and she had never forgotten that if you drink much from a bottle marked "Poison," it is almost certain to make you sick sooner or later.

However, this bottle was *not* marked "Poison," so Alice decided to take a cautious sip. Finding it absolutely delicious (in fact, it tasted like a blend of cherry tart, creamy custard, pineapple, roast turkey, buttery toffee, and hot buttered toast), she quickly finished it off.

"What a strange sensation!" Alice exclaimed. "I must be collapsing like a telescope!"

And so she was, indeed! She was now only ten inches tall, and her face lit up at the thought that she was finally the right size to go through the little door into that lovely garden. First, though, she waited a few minutes to see if she was going to shrink any further. She felt a bit nervous about this. "Because it might end up," Alice said to herself, "with me going out completely, like a candle! I wonder what I'd be like then?" And she tried to imagine what the flame of a candle looks like after it's blown out, though she couldn't remember ever seeing such a thing.

After a while, finding that nothing more was happening, she decided to head for the garden at once. But oh, poor Alice! When she got to the door, she realized she had forgotten the little golden key. When she went back to the table for it, she found that she couldn't possibly reach it! She could see it quite clearly through the glass, and she tried her hardest to scramble up one of the table legs, but it was far too slippery. When she had completely exhausted herself with trying, the poor little thing sat down on the floor and cried.

"Come on now, there's no use in crying like that!" Alice told herself rather sharply. "I advise you to stop this instant!" She generally gave herself very good advice (even if she rarely followed it), and sometimes she scolded herself so sternly that she brought tears to her own eyes. Once, she remembered trying to box her own ears for cheating in a game of croquet she was playing against herself, because this quirky child was extraordinarily fond of pretending to be two people. "Though it's completely useless now," poor Alice thought, "to pretend to be two people! Why, there's barely enough of me left to make *one* respectable person!"

Soon, her eyes fell on a little glass box lying under the table. She opened it and found a very small cake inside, on which the words **EAT ME** were beautifully spelled out in currants. "Well, I'll eat it," said Alice, "and if it makes me grow bigger, I can reach the key. If it makes me grow smaller, I can creep under the door. So either way, I'll get into the garden, and I don't care which happens!"

She took a small bite and said anxiously to herself, "Which way? Which way?" holding her hand on top of her head to feel which direction she was growing. She was quite surprised to find that she stayed exactly the same size. Of course, that's what usually happens when someone eats cake, but Alice had gotten so used to expecting nothing but extraordinary things to happen that it seemed terribly dull and foolish for life to go on in the ordinary way.

So she set to work, and very quickly polished off the rest of the cake.
