# Chapter 5: Advice from a Caterpillar

The Caterpillar and Alice looked at each other in silence for quite a while. At last, the Caterpillar took the hookah pipe out of its mouth and spoke to her in a slow, sleepy, drawling voice.

"Who are *you*?" asked the Caterpillar.

This wasn't exactly an encouraging start to a conversation. Alice replied rather shyly, "I—I honestly don't know right now, sir. At least, I know who I *was* when I woke up this morning, but I think I must have changed several times since then."

"What do you mean by that?" the Caterpillar asked sternly. "Explain yourself!"

"I'm afraid I can't explain *myself*, sir," Alice said, "because I'm not myself, you see."

"I don't see at all," said the Caterpillar.

"I'm afraid I can't put it any more clearly," Alice replied very politely, "because I don't understand it myself to begin with. And changing into so many different sizes in a single day is terribly confusing."

"No, it isn't," said the Caterpillar.

"Well, maybe you haven't experienced it yet," Alice said. "But when you have to turn into a chrysalis—and you will someday, you know—and then after that into a butterfly, I'd imagine you'll feel a bit strange, won't you?"

"Not one bit," said the Caterpillar.

"Well, maybe you feel differently," Alice said. "All I know is, it would feel very strange to *me*."

"You!" the Caterpillar said scornfully. "Who are *you*?"

That brought them right back to the beginning of the conversation. Alice was starting to feel rather annoyed by the Caterpillar's snappy, one-line answers. Drawing herself up, she said very seriously, "I think you ought to tell me who *you* are first."

"Why?" asked the Caterpillar.

Here was another baffling question. Since Alice couldn't think of a single good reason, and the Caterpillar seemed to be in an exceptionally foul mood, she turned to walk away.

"Come back!" the Caterpillar called after her. "I have something important to say!"

That certainly sounded promising. Alice turned around and walked back.

"Keep your temper," said the Caterpillar.

"Is that all?" Alice asked, swallowing down her annoyance as best as she could.

"No," said the Caterpillar.

Alice thought she might as well wait. She had nothing else to do, and maybe, just maybe, it would tell her something worth hearing. For a few minutes, it puffed away in silence. At last, it unfolded its arms, took the pipe from its mouth again, and asked, "So you think you've changed, do you?"

"I'm afraid so, sir," said Alice. "I can't remember things the way I used to—and I can't stay the same size for ten minutes in a row!"

"Can't remember *what* things?" asked the Caterpillar.

"Well, I tried to recite 'How doth the little busy bee,' but all the words came out totally wrong!" Alice replied in a mournful voice.

"Recite: '*You are old, Father William*,'" the Caterpillar commanded.

Alice folded her hands and began:

> "You are old, Father William," the young man said,  
> "And your hair has turned totally white;  
> Yet you constantly stand on your head upside down—  
> Do you think, at your age, that is right?"  
>  
> "In my youth," Father William replied to his son,  
> "I feared it might injure my brain;  
> But now that I'm perfectly sure I have none,  
> Why, I do it again and again!"  
>  
> "You are old," said the youth, "as I mentioned before,  
> And you've grown most remarkably fat;  
> Yet you flipped a back-somersault in through the door—  
> Now what is the reason for that?"  
>  
> "In my youth," said the sage, as he shook his gray locks,  
> "I kept all my joints loose and supple  
> By using this ointment—ten dollars a box—  
> Allow me to sell you a couple?"  
>  
> "You are old," said the youth, "and your jaws are too weak  
> For anything tougher than suet;  
> Yet you finished the goose, with the bones and the beak—  
> Just how did you manage to do it?"  
>  
> "In my youth," said his father, "I studied the law,  
> And argued each case with my wife;  
> And the muscular strength that it gave to my jaw  
> Has lasted the rest of my life."  
>  
> "You are old," said the youth, "one would hardly suppose  
> That your eye was as steady as ever;  
> Yet you balanced an eel on the tip of your nose—  
> What made you so terribly clever?"  
>  
> "I have answered three questions, and that is enough!"  
> Said his father. "Don't put on such airs!  
> Do you think I have all day to listen to stuff?  
> Get lost, or I'll kick you downstairs!"

"You didn't say that right," said the Caterpillar.

"Not *quite* right, I'm afraid," Alice admitted timidly. "Some of the words got mixed up."

"It's wrong from beginning to end," the Caterpillar said decisively. And with that, they sat in silence for several minutes.

The Caterpillar was the first to speak again.

"What size do you want to be?" it asked.

"Oh, I'm not that picky about my size," Alice quickly replied. "It's just that nobody likes changing so often, you know."

"I *don't* know," said the Caterpillar.

Alice didn't say anything. She had never been contradicted so much in all her life, and she could feel herself losing her patience.

"Are you satisfied now?" asked the Caterpillar.

"Well, I'd like to be a *little* taller, sir, if you don't mind," Alice said. "Three inches is such a miserable height to be."

"It is a perfectly fine height!" the Caterpillar snapped, rearing itself straight up as it spoke (it happened to be exactly three inches tall).

"But I'm not used to it!" poor Alice pleaded in a pitiful voice. And she thought to herself, *I wish these creatures wouldn't get offended so easily!*

"You'll get used to it in time," the Caterpillar said. It put the hookah pipe back in its mouth and began smoking again.

This time, Alice waited patiently until it felt like speaking. A minute or two later, the Caterpillar took the pipe out of its mouth, yawned once or twice, and shook itself all over. Then it climbed down off the mushroom and crawled away through the grass, casually remarking as it went, "One side will make you grow taller, and the other side will make you grow shorter."

*One side of what? The other side of what?* Alice thought to herself.

"Of the mushroom," said the Caterpillar, answering as if she had spoken out loud. A moment later, it was completely out of sight.

Alice stood staring thoughtfully at the mushroom for a minute, trying to figure out which were its two sides. Since it was perfectly round, she found this to be a rather tricky puzzle. Still, reaching her arms as far around it as they would go, she snapped off a piece of the edge with each hand.

"And now, which is which?" she murmured. She nibbled a tiny bite from the right-hand piece to test the effect. In the next instant, she felt a violent smack right under her chin—it had slammed straight into her foot!

The sudden change terrified her, but there was no time to lose—she was shrinking fast! She immediately set to work trying to nibble the other piece. Her chin was squished so tightly against her foot that there was barely enough room to open her mouth, but she managed it at last and swallowed a tiny crumb of the left-hand piece.

---

"Yes! My head's finally free!" Alice exclaimed with delight. But her joy instantly turned to alarm when she realized her shoulders were nowhere to be found. All she could see when she looked down was an impossibly long neck, rising like a giant stalk out of a sea of green leaves far below.

"What on earth *is* all that green stuff?" Alice wondered aloud. "And where *have* my shoulders gone? And oh, my poor hands, why can't I see you?" She waved them around as she spoke, but nothing seemed to happen, except for a faint rustling down in the distant canopy of leaves.

Since there seemed to be no hope of bringing her hands up to her head, she tried bringing her head down to her hands. To her delight, her neck bent easily in every direction, just like a snake. She had just managed to curve it into a graceful zigzag and was about to dive into the foliage—which turned out to be the treetops she had been wandering under—when a sharp hiss made her jerk backward. A large pigeon had flown right into her face and was furiously flapping its wings against her.

"Serpent!" screamed the Pigeon.

"I am *not* a serpent!" Alice said indignantly. "Leave me alone!"

"Serpent, I say!" the Pigeon repeated, dropping its voice to an exhausted, sobbing whisper. "I’ve tried everything, and nothing keeps them away!"

"I haven't the faintest idea what you're talking about," Alice said.

"I’ve tried the roots of trees, I’ve tried riverbanks, I’ve tried hedges," the Pigeon went on, paying zero attention to her. "But those serpents! There’s simply no pleasing them!"

Alice grew more and more baffled, but she figured there was no point saying anything else until the Pigeon had finished venting.

"As if hatching eggs wasn't hard enough work already!" the Pigeon complained. "Now I have to keep watch for serpents day and night! I haven't slept a wink in three weeks!"

"I'm so sorry you've been having such a hard time," Alice said, starting to understand.

"And just when I picked the tallest tree in the entire woods," the Pigeon continued, its voice ramping up to a screech, "and just when I thought I was finally safe from them, they have to come slithering down from the sky! Ugh, you serpent!"

"But I'm *not* a serpent, I tell you!" Alice cried. "I'm a... I'm a..."

"Well? *What* are you?" demanded the Pigeon. "I can tell you’re trying to make something up!"

"I—I'm a little girl," Alice said rather hesitantly, remembering just how many changes she had gone through that day.

"A likely story!" scoffed the Pigeon with utter contempt. "I’ve seen plenty of little girls in my time, but not *one* with a neck like that! No, no! You’re a serpent, and there’s no point denying it. I suppose you'll tell me next that you've never tasted an egg!"

"I *have* eaten eggs, of course," said Alice, who was a very honest child. "Little girls eat eggs just as much as serpents do, you know."

"I don’t believe it," said the Pigeon. "And even if they do, that just means they’re a kind of serpent too, as far as I'm concerned."

This was such a startling new perspective that Alice stayed completely silent for a minute or two. That gave the Pigeon the chance to add, "You’re hunting for eggs, I know *that* much. And what difference does it make to me whether you’re a little girl or a serpent?"

"It makes a big difference to *me*," Alice said quickly. "Besides, I’m not looking for eggs at all. And even if I were, I wouldn’t want *yours*—I don’t like raw eggs!"

"Well, get lost then!" the Pigeon snapped sulkily, settling back down into its nest.

Alice ducked down among the trees as best as she could. Her neck kept getting tangled in the branches, forcing her to stop every few moments to untangle herself. After a while, she remembered she was still holding the pieces of mushroom. Working very carefully, she nibbled first on one piece and then on the other, shooting up and shrinking down, until at last she managed to get herself back to her normal height.

It had been so long since she had been anywhere near her regular size that it felt bizarre at first. But after a few minutes she adjusted and started talking to herself again, as usual.

"Well, that’s half my plan accomplished! All these transformations are so baffling—I never know what I’m going to look like from one minute to the next! Still, I’m back to my normal size. The next step is getting into that gorgeous garden. I wonder how in the world I’m going to do that?"

Just as she said this, she stepped out into a clearing with a little house about four feet tall.

*Whoever lives in there,* Alice thought, *it will never do to barge in at this size. I’d scare them half to death!*

So she began nibbling on the right-hand piece of mushroom again, refusing to go anywhere near the house until she had shrunk herself down to just nine inches tall.
