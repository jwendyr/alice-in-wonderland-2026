# Chapter 2: The Pool of Tears

"Curiouser and curiouser!" cried Alice (she was so shocked that for the moment she completely forgot how to speak proper grammar). "Now I'm stretching out like the biggest telescope that ever existed! Goodbye, feet!" (Because when she looked down at her feet, they were so far off they were practically out of sight.) "Oh, my poor little feet, I wonder who will put on your socks and shoes for you now, darlings? I'm sure *I* won't be able to! I'll be way too far away to bother with you. You'll just have to manage as best you can—though I'd better be kind to them," Alice thought, "or maybe they won't walk where I want to go! Let's see: I'll send them a new pair of boots every Christmas."

And she went on planning to herself how she was going to manage it. "They'll have to go by delivery courier," she thought. "How funny it will seem, sending presents to your own feet! And how bizarre the address label will look:

> *Alice's Right Foot, Esq.*  
> *The Hearthrug (near the Fireplace Fender)*  
> *(With Alice's love)*

Oh dear, what utter nonsense I'm talking!"

Just then, her head bumped against the ceiling of the hall. In fact, she was now over nine feet tall! She immediately snatched up the little golden key and hurried over to the garden door.

Poor Alice! All she could manage, lying flat on her side, was to peek through into the garden with a single eye. But squeezing through was more hopeless than ever. She sat down and began to weep all over again.

"You ought to be ashamed of yourself," Alice scolded, "a great big girl like you" (and she certainly had a point!) "crying like this! Stop it this instant, I tell you!" But she went right on crying anyway, shedding gallons of tears until a large pool formed all around her, about four inches deep and stretching halfway down the hall.

After a while, she heard the soft pitter-patter of little feet in the distance, and she hastily wiped her eyes to see what was coming. It was the White Rabbit returning, splendidly dressed, carrying a pair of fancy white kidskin gloves in one hand and a large folding fan in the other. He came trotting along in a tremendous rush, muttering to himself as he came, "Oh! The Duchess, the Duchess! Won't she be furious if I've kept her waiting!" Alice felt so desperate that she was ready to ask anyone for help, so when the Rabbit drew near, she began in a small, timid voice, "Excuse me, sir—" The Rabbit leaped out of his skin, dropped the white gloves and the fan, and scurried off into the darkness as fast as his legs could carry him.

Alice picked up the fan and the gloves, and since the hall was terribly warm, she kept fanning herself as she continued talking to herself: "Good grief! How strange everything is today! And yesterday everything went along just as usual. I wonder if I was transformed during the night? Let me think: was I the same when I got up this morning? I almost feel like I remember feeling a little different. But if I'm not the same, the next question is: who in the world am I? Ah, *that's* the great mystery!" And she began going over all the kids she knew who were her age, to see if she could have been swapped for any of them.

"I'm certain I'm not Ada," she said, "because her hair falls in such long ringlets, and mine doesn't curl at all. And I definitely can't be Mabel, because I know all sorts of things, and she—oh, she knows so very little! Besides, *she's* she, and *I'm* I, and—oh dear, what a puzzle it all is! Let me check if I still know all the things I used to know. Let's see: four times five is twelve, four times six is thirteen, and four times seven is—oh no, I'll never get to twenty at this rate! But never mind the multiplication tables, let's try Geography. London is the capital of Paris, and Paris is the capital of Rome, and Rome—no, *that's* completely wrong, I'm certain! I really must have been turned into Mabel! Let me try reciting 'How doth the little—'" She folded her hands primly on her lap as if she were in the classroom, and began to repeat it, but her voice sounded scratchy and weird, and the words didn't come out the way they used to:

> "How does the little crocodile  
> Improve his shiny tail,  
> And pour the waters of the Nile  
> On every golden scale!  
>  
> How cheerfully he seems to grin,  
> How neatly spreads his claws,  
> And welcomes little fishes in  
> With gently smiling jaws!"  

"I'm positive those aren't the right words," poor Alice said, her eyes filling with tears again as she went on. "I must be Mabel after all, and I'll have to go live in that cramped little house, with practically no toys to play with, and oh, endless lessons to memorize! No, I've made up my mind: if I'm Mabel, I'll stay down here! It won't do them any good to poke their heads down and call, 'Come back up, sweetie!' I'll just look up and say, 'Who am I, then? Tell me that first, and then, if I like being that person, I'll come up. If not, I'll stay down here until I'm somebody else!' But oh..." cried Alice with a sudden burst of tears, "I really wish they *would* poke their heads down! I am so, so tired of being all alone here!"

As she said this, she looked down at her hands and was startled to see that she had slipped on one of the Rabbit's little white kidskin gloves while talking. "How on earth did I do *that*?" she wondered. "I must be shrinking again!" She got up and hurried to the table to measure herself against it, finding that, by her best estimate, she was now only about two feet tall and shrinking fast. She soon realized that the cause was the fan she was holding, and she dropped it instantly—just in the nick of time to avoid shrinking away into nothingness.

"That *was* a close call!" Alice said, thoroughly frightened by the sudden change, but immensely relieved to find herself still in existence. "And now for the garden!" She dashed as fast as she could back to the little door. But alas! The little door was locked tight again, and the tiny golden key was lying on top of the glass table just like before. "And things are worse than ever," the poor child thought, "because I've never been this small before, never! And honestly, it's totally unfair, that's what it is!"

Just as she said these words, her foot slipped, and the next second—*splash!*—she was up to her chin in salty water. Her first thought was that she had somehow fallen into the sea. "And in that case, I can take the train home," she told herself. (Alice had been to the beach once in her life, and had come to the general conclusion that wherever you go along the English coast, you find rows of changing huts out in the water, children digging in the sand with wooden shovels, a line of boarding houses, and a train station right behind them.) Before long, however, she realized the truth: she was swimming in the massive pool of tears she had wept when she was nine feet tall.

"I wish I hadn't cried so much!" Alice said as she paddled around, trying to find her way out. "Now I suppose I'll be punished by drowning in my own tears! That *will* be a bizarre fate, for sure! But then again, absolutely everything is bizarre today."

Just then, she heard something splashing in the pool a little way off, and she swam closer to make out what it was. At first, she thought it must be a walrus or a hippopotamus, but then she remembered how tiny she was now, and she soon realized it was only a mouse that had slipped in just like her.

"Would there be any use," Alice thought, "in trying to talk to this mouse? Everything is so upside down down here that it seems very likely it can talk. At any rate, there's no harm in trying." So she began: "O Mouse, do you know the way out of this pool? I am very tired of swimming around here, O Mouse!" (Alice assumed this must be the proper way to speak to a mouse. She had never spoken to one before, but she remembered seeing the declension in her brother's Latin grammar book: "A mouse—of a mouse—to a mouse—a mouse—O mouse!") The Mouse looked at her rather curiously and seemed to give a tiny wink with one of its little eyes, but it said nothing.

"Maybe it doesn't speak English," Alice thought. "I bet it's a French mouse that came over with William the Conqueror." (For all her knowledge of history, Alice had no clear notion of how long ago anything had actually happened.) So she tried again: "*Où est ma chatte?*" ("Where is my cat?"), which was the first sentence in her French textbook. The Mouse leaped right out of the water in a panic, trembling all over with fright. "Oh, I beg your pardon!" Alice cried hastily, terrified that she had hurt the poor creature's feelings. "I completely forgot you don't like cats!"

"*Not like cats!*" cried the Mouse in a shrill, furious voice. "Would *you* like cats if you were in my place?"

"Well, maybe not," Alice said soothingly. "Please don't be angry about it. And yet, I really wish I could show you our cat Dinah. I think you'd take a liking to cats if you could only see her. She is such a sweet, quiet little thing," Alice continued dreamily as she paddled lazily through the pool. "She curls up purring so nicely by the fire, licking her paws and washing her face, and she's so delightfully soft to cuddle—plus she's an absolute champion at catching mice—oh, I beg your pardon!" Alice cried again, for this time the Mouse was bristling all over, and she felt certain he was deeply offended. "We won't talk about her anymore if you'd rather not."

"*We*, indeed!" squeaked the Mouse, who was trembling right down to the tip of his tail. "As if *I* would ever speak on such a subject! Our family has always *hated* cats—nasty, cruel, vulgar creatures! Don't let me hear that name again!"

"I won't! Truly!" Alice said, in a frantic hurry to change the subject. "Are you... are you fond of... dogs?" The Mouse did not answer, so Alice went on eagerly: "There's such a lovely little dog near our house that I'd love to show you! A spunky little bright-eyed terrier, you know, with oh, such long curly brown hair! He fetches things when you throw them, sits up and begs for his dinner, and does all kinds of tricks—I can't even remember half of them! He belongs to a farmer nearby, who says he's so useful he's worth a fortune! He says the dog kills all the rats and—oh dear!" Alice cried sorrowfully. "I'm afraid I've offended him again!" For the Mouse was paddling away from her as fast as his little legs could go, churning up quite a commotion in the pool as he went.

So she called softly after him, "Dear little Mouse! Please come back, and we won't talk about cats or dogs at all, if you don't like them!" When the Mouse heard this, he turned around and swam slowly back to her. His face was quite pale (from intense emotion, Alice thought), and he said in a low, trembling voice, "Let us get to the shore, and then I will tell you my life story, and you'll understand why it is I hate cats and dogs so much."

It was high time to get out, for the pool was getting quite crowded with all the birds and animals that had fallen into it. There were a Duck and a Dodo, a Lory parrot and an Eaglet, along with several other eccentric creatures. With Alice leading the way, the whole party swam to the shore.
