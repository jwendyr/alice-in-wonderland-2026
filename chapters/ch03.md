# Chapter 3: A Caucus-Race and a Long Tale

It was quite a bizarre-looking crowd that gathered on the riverbank: birds with soaked, drooping feathers, animals with fur plastered flat against their bodies, and everyone dripping wet, cranky, and thoroughly miserable.

The first question, of course, was how to get dry again. They held a conference to discuss it, and after a few minutes, it felt completely natural to Alice to find herself chatting casually with them, as if they had known each other her whole life. In fact, she got into quite a long argument with the Lory, who eventually turned sulky and would only insist, "I am older than you, so I must know better!" But Alice refused to concede that without knowing how old the bird actually was. Since the Lory flatly refused to give its age, the debate came to a complete standstill.

Finally, the Mouse—who seemed to be regarded as a figure of authority among them—called out, "Sit down, all of you, and listen to me! *I’ll* soon make you dry enough!"

Everyone sat down immediately in a wide circle with the Mouse right in the center. Alice kept her eyes nervously glued to it, convinced she would catch a terrible cold if she didn’t dry off very soon.

"Ahem!" the Mouse began with an important air. "Is everyone ready? This is the driest thing I know. Complete silence all around, if you please! 'William the Conqueror, whose cause was favored by the Pope, soon secured the submission of the English, who lacked leaders and had lately grown quite accustomed to usurpation and conquest. Edwin and Morcar, the Earls of Mercia and Northumbria—'"

"Ugh!" groaned the Lory with a shudder.

"I beg your pardon?" the Mouse said, frowning, but maintaining a very polite tone. "Did you speak?"

"Not me!" the Lory replied hastily.

"I thought you did," said the Mouse. "—I proceed. 'Edwin and Morcar, the Earls of Mercia and Northumbria, declared their support for him; and even Stigand, the patriotic Archbishop of Canterbury, found it advisable—'"

"Found *what*?" asked the Duck.

"Found *it*," the Mouse snapped, rather annoyed. "Surely you know what 'it' means!"

"I know what 'it' means well enough when *I* find something," said the Duck. "Usually it’s a frog or a worm. The question is, what did the archbishop find?"

The Mouse ignored the question entirely and pressed on hurriedly: "'—found it advisable to accompany Edgar the Atheling to meet William and offer him the crown. William’s conduct was moderate at first, but the insolence of his Normans—' How are you coming along now, my dear?" it asked, turning toward Alice.

"Just as wet as ever," Alice replied mournfully. "It doesn’t seem to be drying me at all."

"In that case," the Dodo declared solemnly, rising to its feet, "I move that the meeting adjourn for the immediate adoption of more energetic remedies—"

"Speak English!" cut in the Eaglet. "I don’t know what half those big words mean, and what’s more, I don’t believe you do either!" The Eaglet ducked its head to hide a smirk, and several other birds giggled out loud.

"What I was *trying* to say," the Dodo said with offended dignity, "is that the best thing to dry us off would be a Caucus-race."

"What *is* a Caucus-race?" Alice asked. Not that she particularly cared to know, but the Dodo had paused as though expecting *someone* to speak up, and nobody else seemed inclined to say a thing.

"Why," said the Dodo, "the best way to explain it is to just do it." (And in case you’d like to try it yourself on a cold winter day, I’ll tell you how the Dodo ran it.)

First, it marked out a track in a rough circle ("The exact shape doesn't really matter," it explained). Then the whole crowd was placed along the course, scattered here and there. There was no "Ready, set, go!" Everyone simply started running whenever they felt like it and stopped whenever they wanted, so it wasn’t at all easy to know when the race was over.

Still, once they had been running around for about half an hour and were completely dry again, the Dodo suddenly called out, "The race is over!"

They all swarmed around it, panting for breath and asking, "So who won?"

That was a question the Dodo couldn’t answer without some serious thought. It sat for a long time with one finger pressed against its forehead (the classic thinking pose Shakespeare always strikes in portraits), while everyone else waited in silence.

At last, the Dodo announced, "*Everyone* has won, and all must have prizes!"

"But who is giving out the prizes?" quite a chorus of voices asked.

"Why, *she* is, of course," said the Dodo, pointing a finger straight at Alice.

Instantly the entire crowd swarmed around her, shouting in a confused scramble, "Prizes! Prizes!"

Alice had no idea what to do. In desperation, she reached into her pocket and pulled out a box of candies (luckily, the salty water hadn’t gotten inside) and passed them out as prizes. There was exactly one for everyone, all the way around.

"Wait, she has to have a prize herself, you know," said the Mouse.

"Of course," the Dodo replied with great gravity. "What else do you have in your pocket?" it asked, turning to Alice.

"Only a thimble," Alice said sadly.

"Hand it over here," said the Dodo.

Then they all gathered around her once more while the Dodo solemnly presented the thimble, saying, "We beg your acceptance of this elegant thimble." When it finished this short speech, the whole crowd cheered enthusiastically.

Alice thought the entire ceremony was utterly ridiculous, but everyone looked so dead serious that she didn’t dare laugh. Since she couldn’t think of anything to say, she simply bowed, accepted the thimble, and tried to look as solemn as possible.

The next order of business was eating the candies. This created quite a commotion, as the larger birds complained that they couldn’t even taste theirs, while the smaller ones choked and had to be patted on the back. Eventually, the chaos died down, and they all sat in a circle again, begging the Mouse to tell them another story.

"You promised to tell me your history, remember?" Alice said. "And why it is you hate—C and D," she added in a whisper, half-afraid it would take offense again.

"Mine is a long and a sad tale!" the Mouse said, turning to Alice with a heavy sigh.

"It *is* a long tail, certainly," Alice remarked, looking down in wonder at the Mouse’s actual tail. "But why do you call it sad?"

And she kept puzzling over the question while the Mouse was speaking, so in her imagination, her picture of the tale looked something like this:

"You’re not paying attention!" the Mouse scolded Alice severely. "What on earth are you thinking about?"

"I beg your pardon," Alice said very meekly. "You had gotten to the fifth bend, I think?"

"I had *not!*" cried the Mouse, sharp and furious.

"A knot!" said Alice, always eager to make herself useful, looking around anxiously. "Oh, please let me help untie it!"

"I shall do nothing of the kind!" the Mouse snapped, getting to its feet and marching away. "You insult me by talking such nonsense!"

"I didn’t mean to!" poor Alice pleaded. "You’re just so easily offended, you know!"

The Mouse only grumbled in reply.

"Please come back and finish your story!" Alice called after it, and the others all joined in chorus, "Yes, please do!"

But the Mouse only shook its head impatiently and walked even faster.

"What a shame it wouldn’t stay!" sighed the Lory as soon as the Mouse was completely out of sight.

An old Crab took the opportunity to lecture her daughter: "Ah, my dear! Let this be a lesson to you never to lose *your* temper!"

"Quiet, Mom!" snapped the young Crab. "You’d test the patience of an oyster!"

"I wish I had our Dinah here, I really do!" Alice said aloud, addressing nobody in particular. "She’d bring it back in no time!"

"And who is Dinah, if I might venture to ask?" inquired the Lory.

Alice answered eagerly, always ready to talk about her pet: "Dinah is our cat. And she’s so amazing at catching mice, you wouldn’t believe it! And oh, I wish you could see her go after birds! Why, she’ll gobble up a little bird as soon as look at it!"

This speech caused quite a stir among the group. Several birds hurried off immediately. An old Magpie began wrapping itself up very carefully, remarking, "I really must be heading home; the night air doesn’t agree with my throat!" A Canary called out in a trembling voice to its little ones, "Come along, sweethearts! It’s high time you were all in bed!" Under one excuse or another, they all slipped away, and Alice was soon left completely alone.

"I wish I hadn’t brought up Dinah!" she said to herself mournfully. "Nobody down here seems to like her, and I’m sure she’s the best cat in the whole world! Oh, my sweet Dinah! I wonder if I’ll ever see you again!"

And with that, poor Alice began to cry once more, feeling dreadfully lonely and discouraged. Before long, however, she heard the light patter of little footsteps in the distance. She looked up eagerly, half-hoping the Mouse had changed his mind and was coming back to finish his story.
