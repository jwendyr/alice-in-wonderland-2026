# Chapter 10: The Lobster Quadrille

The Mock Turtle sighed heavily and wiped the back of one flipper across his eyes. He looked at Alice and tried to speak, but for a minute or two, choking sobs caught in his throat.

"Sounds like he's choked on a bone," the Gryphon grunted, setting to work shaking him and thumping him on the back.

At last, the Mock Turtle got his voice back. With tears streaming down his cheeks, he continued:

"You might not have spent much time living under the sea—"

("I haven't," Alice chimed in.)

"—and maybe you've never even been formally introduced to a lobster—"

(Alice started to say, "I once tasted—" but caught herself just in time and said, "No, never.")

"—so you couldn't possibly imagine what a marvelous thing a Lobster Quadrille is!"

"I really can't," Alice admitted. "What kind of dance is it?"

"Well," the Gryphon explained, "first you line up along the seashore—"

"*Two* lines!" the Mock Turtle cried. "Seals, turtles, salmon, and all the rest. Then, once you've cleared all the jellyfish out of the way—"

"*That* usually takes a while," the Gryphon interjected.

"—you step forward twice—"

"Each partner paired with a lobster!" shouted the Gryphon.

"Naturally," the Mock Turtle said. "Step forward twice, bow to your partners—"

"—switch lobsters, and step back in the same order," the Gryphon chimed in.

"Then, you see," the Mock Turtle continued, "you chuck the—"

"The lobsters!" the Gryphon hollered, leaping into the air.

"—as far out to sea as you possibly can—"

"Swim after them!" shrieked the Gryphon.

"Do a somersault in the waves!" shouted the Mock Turtle, dancing around frantically.

"Swap lobsters again!" bellowed the Gryphon at the top of its lungs.

"Head back to shore, and that's the end of the first routine," the Mock Turtle concluded, his voice suddenly dropping to a whisper.

The two creatures, who had been bouncing around like wild maniacs, abruptly sat back down, mournful and dead silent, staring at Alice.

"It sounds like a very lovely dance," Alice ventured timidly.

"Would you like to see a bit of it?" asked the Mock Turtle.

"I would love that," Alice replied.

"Come on, let's show her the first routine!" the Mock Turtle said to the Gryphon. "We can manage without lobsters. Who's going to sing?"

"Oh, *you* sing," said the Gryphon. "I've forgotten the words."

So they began dancing solemnly in circles around Alice, stomping on her toes every now and then whenever they passed too close, and waving their front paws to keep time while the Mock Turtle sang this, very slowly and sorrowfully:

> "Won't you walk a little faster?" said a whiting to a snail,  
> "There's a porpoise right behind us, and he's stepping on my tail!  
> Look how eagerly the lobsters and the turtles all advance—  
> They are waiting on the pebbles: will you come and join the dance?  
> Will you, won't you, will you, won't you, will you join the dance?  
> Will you, won't you, will you, won't you, won't you join the dance?  
>  
> "You can really have no notion how delightful it will be  
> When they pick us up and toss us, with the lobsters, out to sea!"  
> But the snail replied, "Too far, too far!" and gave a sideways glance,  
> Saying thanks, but no, he really could not, would not join the dance.  
> Would not, could not, would not, could not, would not join the dance,  
> Would not, could not, would not, could not, could not join the dance!  
>  
> "What does it matter how far we go?" his scaly friend replied,  
> "There's another pleasant shoreline waiting on the other side.  
> The farther off from England, why, the closer into France!  
> So don't turn pale, beloved snail, but come and join the dance!  
> Will you, won't you, will you, won't you, will you join the dance?  
> Will you, won't you, will you, won't you, won't you join the dance?"

"Thank you, that was such a fascinating dance to watch," Alice said, secretly relieved that it had finally ended. "And I really enjoyed that bizarre song about the whiting!"

"Oh, speaking of whitings," said the Mock Turtle, "they—well, you've seen them, haven't you?"

"Yes," Alice replied, "I've often seen them at dinn—" She bit her tongue just in time.

"I haven't the foggiest where Dinn might be," said the Mock Turtle, "but if you've seen them so often, you surely know what they look like."

"I think so," Alice said thoughtfully. "They have their tails in their mouths—and they're coated all over in breadcrumbs."

"You're wrong about the crumbs," said the Mock Turtle. "Breadcrumbs would wash right off in the sea! But they *do* have their tails in their mouths, and the reason is—"

Here the Mock Turtle yawned and closed his eyes.

"—tell her the reason and all that," he mumbled to the Gryphon.

"The reason is," said the Gryphon, "that they *insisted* on joining the lobsters for the dance. So they got tossed out to sea. So they fell a terribly long way. So their tails got stuck fast in their mouths. So they could never pry them back out. End of story."

"Thank you," said Alice. "That's really interesting. I never knew so much about whitings before."

"I can tell you even more than that if you want," said the Gryphon. "Do you know why it's called a whiting?"

"I never really thought about it," said Alice. "Why?"

"*It cleans the boots and shoes*," the Gryphon answered with utter solemnity.

Alice was completely baffled. "Cleans boots and shoes?" she repeated in bewilderment.

"Well, what do you shine *your* shoes with?" asked the Gryphon. "I mean, what makes them so shiny?"

Alice looked down at her shoes and paused to think before answering. "They're polished with blacking, I believe."

"Boots and shoes under the sea," the Gryphon said in a low, know-it-all voice, "are polished with whiting. Now you know."

"And what are they made of?" Alice asked, genuinely curious.

"Soles and eels, obviously!" the Gryphon snapped, losing patience. "Any shrimp could have told you that!"

"If I'd been the whiting," Alice remarked, her mind still wandering back to the song, "I would have told that porpoise, 'Back off, please—we don't want *you* tagging along!'"

"They had no choice but to bring him along," said the Mock Turtle. "No sensible fish would ever travel anywhere without a porpoise."

"Really? Wouldn't it?" Alice asked in surprise.

"Of course not!" said the Mock Turtle. "Why, if a fish came up to *me* and said he was going on a trip, I'd say, 'For what porpoise?'"

"Don't you mean 'purpose'?" asked Alice.

"I say what I mean," the Mock Turtle huffed, sounding quite offended.

The Gryphon jumped in: "Come on, let's hear some of *your* adventures now."

"I could tell you about my adventures, starting from this morning," Alice offered a little tentatively. "There's no point in going back to yesterday, though, because I was an entirely different person back then."

"Explain that!" said the Mock Turtle.

"No, no! Adventures first!" the Gryphon insisted impatiently. "Explanations take such an exhausting amount of time."

So Alice started recounting her adventures, beginning from the moment she first spotted the White Rabbit. She felt quite nervous at first, as both creatures crowded in uncomfortably close—one on each side—staring with their eyes and mouths hanging wide open. But she gathered confidence as she kept talking. Her audience stayed totally silent until she reached the part where she recited "You are old, Father William" to the Caterpillar and the words came out completely scrambled. At that, the Mock Turtle drew a deep breath and murmured, "Well, that is very strange."

"As weird as it gets," agreed the Gryphon.

"The words all came out wrong!" the Mock Turtle repeated thoughtfully. "I'd love to hear her try reciting something right now. Tell her to start." He looked at the Gryphon as though assuming it had official authority over Alice.

"Stand up and recite ''Tis the voice of the sluggard,'" ordered the Gryphon.

*The way these animals boss people around and force them to recite school lessons!* Alice thought. *I might as well be stuck in a classroom.*

Still, she got to her feet and began to recite. But her mind was so swirling with the Lobster Quadrille that she barely knew what was coming out of her mouth, and the words turned out very bizarre indeed:

> "'Tis the voice of the Lobster; I heard him declare:  
> 'You've baked me too brown, I must sugar my hair.'  
> Just as ducks use their eyelids, so he with his nose  
> Adjusts his belt buckles and turns out his toes.  
> When the beaches are dry, he's as bold as a lark,  
> Talking trash with a sneer of his neighbor the Shark;  
> But as soon as the tide rolls and sharks swim around,  
> His bold voice turns shaky—a terrified sound."

"That's definitely different from what *I* used to recite as a kid," remarked the Gryphon.

"Well, I've never heard it before," said the Mock Turtle, "but it sounds like extraordinary nonsense."

Alice said nothing. She slumped back down with her face buried in her hands, wondering if anything would *ever* make normal sense again.

"I'd like to have that explained," said the Mock Turtle.

"She can't explain it," the Gryphon said quickly. "Move on to the next verse."

"But what about his toes?" the Mock Turtle pressed on. "How on earth *could* he turn them out with his nose, you know?"

"It's the first position in ballet," Alice offered, but she was completely bewildered by the whole thing and desperate to change the subject.

"Get on with the next verse," the Gryphon urged impatiently. "It starts: 'I passed by his garden.'"

Alice didn't dare disobey, even though she was certain it would turn out all wrong. In a shaky voice, she continued:

> "I walked by his garden and saw with one eye  
> How the Owl and the Panther were sharing a pie:  
> The Panther took pie crust, and gravy, and meat,  
> While the Owl had the dish as his share of the treat.  
> When the pie was all finished, the Owl, as a boon,  
> Was kindly permitted to pocket the spoon,  
> While the Panther claimed knife and fork back with a growl,  
> And concluded the banquet—"

"What is the point of reciting all that stuff," the Mock Turtle cut in, "if you don't explain it as you go along? It's easily the most confusing thing *I've* ever heard!"

"Yeah, you'd better stop there," the Gryphon agreed, and Alice was only too happy to quit.

"Shall we try another routine of the Lobster Quadrille?" the Gryphon asked. "Or would you prefer the Mock Turtle to sing you a song?"

"Oh, a song, please, if the Mock Turtle wouldn't mind!" Alice answered, sounding so enthusiastic that the Gryphon grumbled in a slightly offended tone: "Hmph! There's no accounting for taste! Sing her 'Turtle Soup,' will you, old boy?"

The Mock Turtle let out a heavy sigh and, in a voice that broke with sobs every few seconds, began to sing:

> "Beautiful Soup, so rich and green,  
> Piping hot in a steaming tureen!  
> Who wouldn't bow for a savory scoop?  
> Soup of the evening, beautiful Soup!  
> Soup of the evening, beautiful Soup!  
> &nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Beau—ootiful Soo—oop!  
> &nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Beau—ootiful Soo—oop!  
> Soo—oop of the e—e—evening,  
> &nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Beautiful, beautiful Soup!  
>  
> "Beautiful Soup! Who cares for fish,  
> Roasted poultry, or any other dish?  
> Who wouldn't trade all they have for a scoop,  
> For pennies' worth only of beautiful Soup?  
> Pennies' worth only of beautiful Soup?  
> &nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Beau—ootiful Soo—oop!  
> &nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Beau—ootiful Soo—oop!  
> Soo—oop of the e—e—evening,  
> &nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Beautiful, beauti—FUL SOUP!"

"Chorus again!" cried the Gryphon. The Mock Turtle had only just started to repeat it when a shout echoed in the distance: "The trial is starting!"

"Come on!" shouted the Gryphon. Grabbing Alice by the hand, it dashed off without waiting for the end of the song.

"What trial is it?" Alice panted as she ran.

The Gryphon only yelled, "Come on!" and sprinted even faster, while drifting fainter and fainter on the breeze behind them came those mournful words:

> "Soo—oop of the e—e—evening,  
> &nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Beautiful, beautiful Soup!"
