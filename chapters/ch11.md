# Chapter 11: Who Stole the Tarts?

When they arrived, the King and Queen of Hearts were seated on their thrones, surrounded by an enormous crowd—all sorts of small birds and animals, as well as the entire deck of cards. The Knave stood before them in chains, guarded by a soldier on either side. Near the King stood the White Rabbit, holding a trumpet in one hand and a parchment scroll in the other. Right in the middle of the courtroom sat a table bearing a large platter of tarts. They looked so delicious that just staring at them made Alice's mouth water.

"I really wish they'd hurry up and finish the trial," she thought, "and start passing around the refreshments!" But there didn't seem to be any chance of that happening anytime soon, so she began looking around at everything to pass the time.

Alice had never set foot in a court of law before, but she had read about them in books, and she was delighted to find that she recognized almost everything there.

"That's the judge," she told herself, "because of his big powdered wig."

The judge, as it turned out, was the King. And since he was wearing his royal crown perched directly over the wig (if you're wondering how he managed that, picture a heavy gold crown balanced precariously on a mountain of curls), he didn't look comfortable in the least—and it certainly wasn't a flattering look.

"And that's the jury box," thought Alice, "and those twelve creatures"—she had to say "creatures," you see, because some were animals and some were birds—"I suppose they're the jurors." She repeated the word to herself two or three times, feeling quite proud of knowing it. She figured—and rightly so—that very few girls her age knew what a juror even was. (Though calling them "jurymen" would have worked just as well.)

The twelve jurors were all writing away furiously on chalk slates.

"What are they doing?" Alice whispered to the Gryphon. "They can't possibly have anything to write down yet—the trial hasn't even started!"

"They're writing down their own names," the Gryphon whispered back, "in case they forget them before the trial is over."

"How ridiculous!" Alice blurted out in a loud, indignant voice. But she stopped herself abruptly, because the White Rabbit shouted, "Silence in the court!" and the King adjusted his spectacles and peered anxiously around the room to see who had spoken.

Alice could see, just as clearly as if she were peering right over their shoulders, that every single juror was scribbling "How ridiculous!" onto their slates. She could even tell that one of them didn't know how to spell "ridiculous" and had to nudge his neighbor to ask.

"What an absolute mess their slates will be in before this trial is over!" thought Alice.

One of the jurors had a pencil that squeaked horribly. Naturally, Alice could *not* stand the sound. She crept around the courtroom, slipped up right behind him, and quickly found an opportunity to snatch it away. She was so quick that the poor little juror—who happened to be Bill the Lizard—had no idea what had happened to it. After hunting everywhere in confusion, he was forced to write with one finger for the rest of the day. That wasn't much help, of course, since a finger leaves no mark on a slate.

"Herald, read the charges!" commanded the King.

At this, the White Rabbit blew three sharp blasts on his trumpet, unrolled the parchment scroll, and read aloud:

> "The Queen of Hearts, she baked some tarts,  
> All on a summer day;  
> The Knave of Hearts, he stole those tarts,  
> And took them clean away!"

"Consider your verdict," the King instructed the jury.

"Not yet, not yet!" the White Rabbit interrupted in a hurry. "There's an awful lot to do before we get to that!"

"Call the first witness," said the King.

The White Rabbit blew three blasts on the trumpet and called out, "First witness!"

The first witness was the Hatter. He hurried in holding a teacup in one hand and a slice of buttered bread in the other.

"I beg your pardon, Your Majesty," he began, "for bringing these in, but I hadn't quite finished my tea when I was summoned."

"You should have finished," said the King. "When did you start?"

The Hatter glanced at the March Hare, who had followed him into the courtroom arm-in-arm with the Dormouse.

"The fourteenth of March, I *think* it was," he said.

"The fifteenth," corrected the March Hare.

"The sixteenth," added the Dormouse.

"Write that down," the King told the jury.

The jurors eagerly jotted down all three dates on their slates, added them together, and then converted the total into pounds and pence.

"Take off your hat," the King ordered the Hatter.

"It isn't mine," said the Hatter.

"*Stolen!*" gasped the King, turning to the jury, who instantly made a note of the theft.

"I keep them to sell," the Hatter explained. "None of them belong to me. I'm a hatter, you see."

At that, the Queen put on her spectacles and began glaring intently at the Hatter, who turned pale and started fidgeting.

"Give your testimony," said the King, "and don't be nervous, or I'll have you executed on the spot."

This didn't encourage the witness in the slightest. He kept shifting from one foot to the other, glancing nervously at the Queen. In his utter panic, he took a huge bite right out of his teacup instead of his bread and butter.

Right at that moment, Alice felt a very strange sensation. It puzzled her for several seconds until she realized what was happening: she was beginning to grow larger again! At first, she thought about getting up and walking out of the courtroom, but on second thought, she decided to stay right where she was as long as there was still room for her.

"I wish you wouldn't crowd me so much," said the Dormouse, who was sitting right beside her. "I can barely breathe."

"I can't help it," Alice said meekly. "I'm growing."

"You've got no right to grow *here*," said the Dormouse.

"Don't be ridiculous," Alice replied more boldly. "You know perfectly well you're growing too."

"Yes, but *I* grow at a reasonable pace," said the Dormouse, "not in that absurd, runaway fashion!" He got up in a huff and stomped across to the other side of the courtroom.

The entire time, the Queen hadn't taken her eyes off the Hatter. Just as the Dormouse crossed the room, she told one of the court officers, "Bring me the list of singers from the last concert!"

Hearing this, the wretched Hatter trembled so hard that both of his shoes shook right off his feet.

"Give your testimony!" the King barked angrily. "Or I'll have you executed whether you're nervous or not!"

"I'm a poor man, Your Majesty," the Hatter began in a shaking voice, "—and I hadn't begun my tea—not for more than a week or so—and what with the bread and butter getting so thin—and the twinkling of the tea—"

"The twinkling of the *what?*" asked the King.

"It *began* with the tea," the Hatter stammered.

"Of course twinkling begins with a T!" snapped the King. "Do you take me for an idiot? Keep going!"

"I'm a poor man," the Hatter continued, "and pretty much everything twinkled after that—except the March Hare said—"

"I didn't!" the March Hare interrupted in a frantic hurry.

"You did!" said the Hatter.

"I deny it completely!" insisted the March Hare.

"He denies it," said the King. "Strike that part from the record."

"Well, at any rate, the Dormouse said—" the Hatter went on, glancing around anxiously to see if he would deny it too. But the Dormouse didn't deny a thing, being fast asleep.

"After that," continued the Hatter, "I sliced some more bread and butter—"

"But what did the Dormouse say?" asked one of the jurors.

"That part I can't remember," confessed the Hatter.

"You *must* remember," the King warned him, "or I'll have you executed."

The miserable Hatter dropped his teacup and bread and butter, sinking down onto one knee.

"I'm a poor man, Your Majesty," he whimpered.

"You're a *very* poor *speaker*," remarked the King.

At this, one of the guinea pigs cheered—and was instantly suppressed by the courtroom bailiffs. (Since "suppressed" is a rather formal legal word, let me explain how they did it: they had a large canvas sack with drawstrings at the opening. They slipped the guinea pig inside headfirst, pulled the strings tight, and sat on it.)

"I'm glad I finally saw how that's done," thought Alice. "I've so often read in the papers at the end of trials, 'There was a brief attempt at applause, which was immediately suppressed by the officers of the court,' and I never quite understood what it meant until now!"

"If that's all you know about the matter, you may step down," the King continued.

"I can't get any lower," said the Hatter. "I'm already kneeling on the floor."

"Then you may *sit* down," the King replied.

At that, the other guinea pig cheered—and was promptly suppressed too.

"Well, that takes care of the guinea pigs!" thought Alice. "Now things should run much smoother."

"I'd really rather finish my tea," said the Hatter, eyeing the Queen nervously as she scanned the list of singers.

"You may go," said the King.

The Hatter bolted out of the courtroom so fast he didn't even wait to put his shoes back on.

"—And just take his head off outside!" the Queen shouted after him to one of the guards. But the Hatter was already long gone before the guard could even reach the door.

"Call the next witness!" said the King.

The next witness was the Duchess's cook. She marched in carrying a massive pepper shaker, and Alice guessed who it was before she even stepped inside, thanks to everyone near the doorway sneezing all at once.

"Give your testimony," said the King.

"Nope," said the cook.

The King looked nervously at the White Rabbit, who whispered in a low voice, "Your Majesty will have to cross-examine *this* witness."

"Well, if I must, I suppose I must," the King sighed with a gloomy air. Folding his arms and squinting fiercely at the cook until his eyes were practically shut, he asked in a deep voice, "What are tarts made of?"

"Pepper, mostly," said the cook.

"Molasses," mumbled a sleepy voice behind her.

"Grab that Dormouse!" shrieked the Queen. "Behead that Dormouse! Throw that Dormouse out of court! Suppress him! Pinch him! Cut off his whiskers!"

For several chaotic minutes, the entire courtroom was in total pandemonium while everyone scrambled to throw the Dormouse out. By the time everyone settled back down, the cook had vanished completely.

"Oh, never mind!" said the King, looking enormously relieved. "Call the next witness." Then he leaned over and whispered to the Queen, "Really, my dear, *you* must cross-examine the next one. It's giving me a splitting headache!"

Alice watched the White Rabbit fumble through the witness list, eager to see who could possibly be called next. "They haven't gathered much evidence *yet*," she thought to herself.

Imagine her utter shock when the White Rabbit called out at the top of his shrill little voice:

"Alice!"
