# Chapter 12: Alice's Evidence

"Here!" cried Alice.

In the excitement of the moment, she completely forgot just how enormous she had grown over the last few minutes. She leaped to her feet in such a rush that the hem of her skirt caught the jury box, flipping it completely upside down. All twelve jurors tumbled head over heels onto the crowd below, sprawling everywhere and flapping about helplessly. The sight reminded Alice vividly of a bowl of goldfish she had accidentally knocked over the week before.

"Oh, I am *so* sorry!" she gasped in utter dismay. She immediately began scooping them up as fast as she could. The memory of the spilled goldfish kept racing through her mind, giving her the frantic, illogical impression that if the jurors weren't gathered up and put back in their box immediately, they would die.

"The trial cannot proceed," the King declared in a solemn, heavy voice, "until every single juror is back in their proper place—*every last one*." He stared hard at Alice as he spoke, emphasizing the point.

Alice looked down into the jury box and noticed that, in her haste, she had jammed Bill the Lizard in upside down. The poor little fellow was waving his tail pathetically in the air, completely unable to budge. She quickly lifted him out and turned him right-side up.

"Not that it makes much difference," she thought to herself. "I imagine he'll be *just* as useful to the trial one way up as the other."

As soon as the jury had recovered from the shock of being tipped over, and their slates and pencils had been rounded up and handed back, they went diligently to work writing out an official history of the accident. All except Bill the Lizard, that is—he seemed far too overwhelmed to do anything except sit with his mouth hanging open, staring blankly up at the courtroom ceiling.

"What do you know about this business?" the King asked Alice.

"Nothing," said Alice.

"Nothing *whatever?*" persisted the King.

"Nothing whatever," said Alice.

"That's very important," the King told the jury.

The jurors were just beginning to write this down on their slates when the White Rabbit interrupted.

"*Un*important, Your Majesty means, of course," the Rabbit said in a very respectful tone, though he was frowning and making frantic faces at the King as he spoke.

"*Un*important, of course! That's what I meant," the King hastily corrected himself. Then he went on muttering under his breath, "Important... unimportant... unimportant... important..." as if trying to see which word sounded best.

Some of the jurors wrote down "important," while others wrote "unimportant." Alice could see this clearly because she was close enough to look down over their slates. "Not that it matters in the slightest," she thought.

Just then, the King, who had been scribbling away in his notebook for several minutes, barked out, "Silence!" He adjusted his glasses and read aloud from his book:

"Rule Forty-Two: *All persons more than a mile high must immediately leave the court.*"

Everyone turned to stare at Alice.

"*I'm* not a mile high," said Alice.

"You are," said the King.

"Nearly two miles high, in fact," the Queen chimed in.

"Well, I'm not leaving either way," said Alice. "Besides, that's not a real rule—you just made it up this very second!"

"It's the oldest rule in the book!" the King protested.

"Then it ought to be Rule Number One," said Alice.

The King turned pale and slammed his notebook shut in a hurry.

"Consider your verdict," he told the jury in a low, trembling voice.

"There's more evidence to come yet, if it please Your Majesty!" shouted the White Rabbit, jumping up in a great hurry. "This paper was just picked up off the floor."

"What's in it?" demanded the Queen.

"I haven't opened it yet," said the White Rabbit, "but it appears to be a letter written by the prisoner to... to somebody."

"It must be that," agreed the King, "unless it was written to nobody, which isn't very common, you know."

"Who is it addressed to?" asked one of the jurors.

"It isn't addressed to anyone," said the White Rabbit. "In fact, there's nothing written on the *outside* at all." Unfolding the paper as he spoke, he added, "Wait, it isn't a letter after all—it's a set of verses!"

"Is it in the prisoner's handwriting?" asked another juror.

"No, it isn't," said the White Rabbit, "and that's the strangest thing about it." (The jury all looked thoroughly puzzled.)

"He must have imitated somebody else's handwriting!" said the King. (At this, the jurors' faces all brightened up again.)

"Please, Your Majesty," pleaded the Knave, "I didn't write it, and they can't prove I did! There's no signature at the end."

"If you didn't sign it," said the King, "that only makes things worse! You *must* have meant some mischief, or else you would have signed your name like an honest man."

A general round of applause broke out at this. It was easily the cleverest thing the King had said all day.

"That *proves* his guilt!" said the Queen.

"It proves nothing of the sort!" Alice said firmly. "Why, you don't even know what the verses are about!"

"Read them," said the King.

The White Rabbit put on his spectacles. "Where should I begin, Your Majesty?" he asked.

"Begin at the beginning," the King said gravely, "and go on until you come to the end: then stop."

These were the verses the White Rabbit read:

> "They told me you had been to her,  
> And mentioned me to him:  
> She gave me a good character,  
> But said I could not swim.  
>  
> He sent them word I had not gone  
> (We know it to be true):  
> If she should push the matter on,  
> What would become of you?  
>  
> I gave her one, they gave him two,  
> You gave us three or more;  
> They all returned from him to you,  
> Though they were mine before.  
>  
> If I or she should chance to be  
> Involved in this affair,  
> He trusts to you to set them free,  
> Exactly as we were.  
>  
> My notion was that you had been  
> (Before she had this fit)  
> An obstacle that came between  
> Him, and ourselves, and it.  
>  
> Don’t let him know she liked them best,  
> For this must ever be  
> A secret, kept from all the rest,  
> Between yourself and me."

"That's the most important piece of evidence we've heard yet," said the King, rubbing his hands together. "So now let the jury—"

"If any one of them can explain it," said Alice—she had grown so huge in the last few minutes that she wasn't the least bit afraid of interrupting him—"I'll give them a dollar! *I* don't believe there's a single shred of meaning in it."

Every juror wrote down on their slates, "*She* doesn't believe there's a single shred of meaning in it," but not one of them attempted to explain the paper.

"If there's no meaning in it," said the King, "that saves a world of trouble, you know, since we don't have to try and find any. And yet... I don't know," he went on, spreading the paper out on his knee and squinting at it with one eye. "I seem to see some meaning in it after all. '*...Said I could not swim...*' You can't swim, can you?" he asked, turning to the Knave.

The Knave shook his head mournfully. "Do I look like I can?" he asked. (Which he certainly did *not*, being made entirely of cardboard.)

"Checks out so far," muttered the King, reading through the lines to himself: "'*We know it to be true*—' that's the jury, of course. '*I gave her one, they gave him two*—' why, that must be what he did with the tarts, you see—"

"Hold on, it goes on: '*They all returned from him to you,*'" said Alice.

"Why, there they are!" shouted the King triumphantly, pointing to the tarts on the table. "Nothing could be clearer than *that*! And then again—'*before she had this fit*—' You never had fits, my dear, did you?" he asked the Queen.

"Never!" roared the Queen furiously, throwing a heavy inkstand at the Lizard as she spoke. (Unfortunate little Bill had stopped writing on his slate with his finger since it left no mark, but he hurriedly began again, using the ink trickling down his face for as long as it lasted.)

"Then the words don't *fit* you," said the King, beaming around the courtroom with a proud smile.

Dead silence.

"It's a pun!" the King added in an offended tone.

At once, everybody burst out laughing.

"Let the jury consider their verdict," the King commanded, for about the twentieth time that day.

"No, no!" said the Queen. "Sentence first—verdict afterwards!"

"That's pure nonsense!" Alice said loudly. "The idea of having the sentence first!"

"Hold your tongue!" the Queen shrieked, turning purple.

"I won't!" said Alice.

"Off with her head!" the Queen shouted at the top of her voice.

Nobody moved.

"Who cares what you say?" said Alice. (She had grown back to her full normal size by this time.) "You're nothing but a pack of cards!"

At this, the entire deck rose up into the air and came flying down upon her in a swirling swarm. Alice gave a little scream—half in fright and half in anger—and tried to beat them off...

...Only to find herself lying on the grassy riverbank, her head in the lap of her sister, who was gently brushing away a few dry leaves that had fluttered down from the trees onto her face.

"Wake up, Alice dear!" said her sister. "Why, what a long sleep you've had!"

"Oh, I've had such a strange dream!" said Alice.

And right there by the river, she recounted to her sister, as well as she could remember them, all the bizarre adventures you've just been reading about. When she had finished, her sister kissed her forehead and said, "It *was* a strange dream, dear, certainly. But now run inside for your tea—it's getting late."

So Alice jumped up and ran off, thinking the whole way about what a wonderful dream it had been.

Her sister sat still just as Alice had left her, resting her chin on her hand, watching the setting sun, and thinking of little Alice and all her extraordinary adventures, until she too began dreaming in her own way. And this was her dream:

First, she dreamed of little Alice herself. Once again, those tiny hands were clasped gently upon her knee, and the bright, eager eyes were looking up into hers. She could hear the lively tones of Alice's voice and see that endearing little flick of her head to keep back the stray wisps of hair that *would* always get into her eyes. And still, as she listened—or seemed to listen—the whole world around her became alive with the wondrous creatures of her little sister's dream.

The tall grass rustled at her feet as the White Rabbit hurried by. The frightened Mouse splashed his way through the ripples of the nearby pool. She could hear the clatter of teacups as the March Hare and his friends shared their never-ending meal, and the piercing voice of the Queen shouting orders to execute her unfortunate guests. Once again, the baby-turned-pig was sneezing on the Duchess's knee while plates and platters crashed all around. Once more the shriek of the Gryphon, the squeaking of the Lizard's slate pencil, and the muffled gasps of the suppressed guinea pigs filled the air, mingled with the distant sobs of the miserable Mock Turtle.

So she sat on with closed eyes, half-believing herself in Wonderland, though she knew she had only to open them again and everything would snap back to ordinary reality. The grass would be only rustling in the breeze, and the pool rippling with the sway of the reeds. The rattling teacups would turn into the soft tinkling of sheep bells, and the Queen's shrill cries into the distant calls of a shepherd boy. The baby's sneezes, the Gryphon's shriek, and all the other strange noises would dissolve into the familiar bustle of the busy farmyard, while the lowing of cattle in the distance would take the place of the Mock Turtle's sorrowful sobs.

Lastly, she pictured how this same little sister of hers would, in the years to come, grow into a woman of her own. She knew Alice would keep, throughout all her grown-up years, the simple, loving heart of her childhood. She pictured Alice gathering other little children around her, making *their* eyes shine bright and eager with many a fantastical tale—perhaps even the dream of Wonderland from long ago. She would feel with all their simple sorrows, and find pure pleasure in all their simple joys, remembering her own childhood and the happy summer days.

**THE END**
