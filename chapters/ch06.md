# Chapter 6: Pig and Pepper

For a minute or two, Alice stood looking at the house, wondering what to do next. Suddenly, a footman in fancy uniform came running out of the woods. (She assumed he was a footman because of the uniform; otherwise, judging purely by his face, she would have taken him for a fish.) He rapped loudly on the door with his knuckles.

The door was opened by another footman in uniform, who had a round face and huge, bulging frog eyes. Alice noticed that both footmen had powdered wigs that curled all over their heads. Overcome with curiosity to know what was going on, she crept a little way out from the woods to listen.

The Fish-Footman began by pulling an enormous letter from under his arm—it was nearly as big as he was—and handed it over to the other, announcing in a solemn voice, "For the Duchess. An invitation from the Queen to play croquet."

The Frog-Footman repeated in the exact same grave tone, merely flipping the order of the words around: "From the Queen. An invitation for the Duchess to play croquet."

Then both of them bowed so low that their powdered curls got hopelessly tangled together.

Alice burst out laughing at this and had to duck back into the trees for fear they would hear her. When she peeked out again, the Fish-Footman was gone, and the Frog-Footman was sitting on the ground near the door, staring blankly up at the sky.

Alice walked timidly up to the door and knocked.

"There’s not the slightest use in knocking," the Footman remarked, "and that's for two reasons. First, because I’m on the same side of the door as you are. Second, because they’re making such a racket inside that nobody could possibly hear you."

And there certainly *was* an extraordinary racket going on inside: a nonstop chorus of howling and sneezing, punctuated every few seconds by a tremendous crash, as if another dish or kettle had been smashed to pieces.

"Then please," said Alice, "how am I supposed to get in?"

"There might be some sense in your knocking," the Footman went on, ignoring her completely, "if we had the door between us. For instance, if you were *inside*, you could knock, and I could let you out."

He kept staring straight up into the sky the entire time he spoke, which Alice thought was downright rude. *Maybe he can’t help it,* she thought to herself. *His eyes are practically on top of his head. Still, he could at least answer a question.*

"How am I supposed to get in?" she repeated, louder this time.

"I’m going to sit right here," the Footman observed, "until tomorrow—"

At that exact moment, the front door flew open and a large plate came skimming out, straight at the Footman’s head. It grazed his nose and shattered to pieces against a tree behind him.

"—or the next day, maybe," the Footman continued in the exact same tone, just as if nothing had happened.

"How am I to get in?" Alice asked again, raising her voice.

"*Are* you supposed to get in at all?" said the Footman. "That’s the first question, you know."

It certainly was, but Alice hated being told so. "It’s really maddening," she muttered to herself, "the way every creature here argues. It’s enough to drive anyone crazy!"

The Footman apparently thought this was an ideal moment to repeat himself, with variations. "I shall sit here," he said, "on and off, for days and days."

"But what am *I* supposed to do?" asked Alice.

"Whatever you like," said the Footman, and began whistling.

"Oh, it's completely useless talking to him," Alice groaned in frustration. "He's a total idiot!"

And with that, she opened the door and walked right in.

The door led straight into a large kitchen, which was thick with smoke from one end to the other. In the middle of the room, the Duchess sat on a three-legged stool nursing a baby. Over by the fire, the cook was leaning over a massive cauldron, stirring what looked like a giant batch of soup.

*There is definitely way too much pepper in that soup!* Alice thought to herself, coughing and sneezing as best as she could speak.

There was certainly way too much of it in the air. Even the Duchess sneezed every now and then; and as for the baby, it took turns sneezing and howling at the top of its lungs without a single second's pause. The only two beings in the entire kitchen who weren't sneezing were the cook and a large cat sitting by the hearth, grinning from ear to ear.

"Excuse me, could you please tell me," Alice asked rather timidly—she wasn't quite sure whether good manners allowed her to speak first—"why your cat grins like that?"

"It's a Cheshire Cat," said the Duchess, "and that's why. Pig!"

She shouted that last word with such sudden violence that Alice jumped in surprise. But in the next second, Alice realized the insult was aimed at the baby, not at her. Gathering her courage, she tried again:

"I didn't know Cheshire cats always grinned. In fact, I didn't know cats *could* grin."

"Every cat can," said the Duchess, "and most of them do."

"I don't know of any that do," Alice said very politely, quite pleased to have struck up a conversation.

"You don't know much," snapped the Duchess, "and that's a fact."

Alice didn't like the tone of that remark at all, and decided it would be wise to change the subject. But while she was trying to think of one, the cook hauled the cauldron of soup off the fire and immediately began hurling everything within arm's reach at the Duchess and the baby.

First came the fire irons—the tongs and the poker—followed by a storm of saucepans, plates, and dishes. The Duchess paid them no attention whatsoever, even when they struck her directly. As for the baby, it was already howling so loudly that it was impossible to tell whether the blows hurt it or not.

"Oh, *please* watch what you're doing!" cried Alice, jumping up and down in pure terror. "Oh, watch out—there goes his *poor* little nose!" she shrieked, as an unusually large saucepan whizzed past the baby, very nearly taking its nose clean off.

"If everybody just minded their own business," the Duchess growled hoarsely, "the world would spin a whole lot faster than it does."

"Which would *not* be a good thing," said Alice, thrilled for an opportunity to show off a little of her knowledge. "Just think of the chaos it would create with day and night! You see, the Earth takes twenty-four hours to rotate on its axis—"

"Speaking of axes," the Duchess interrupted, "chop off her head!"

Alice glanced anxiously over at the cook to see if she intended to follow the order. But the cook was busily stirring the soup and didn't seem to be listening. Alice pressed on: "Twenty-four hours, I *think*... or is it twelve? I—"

"Oh, don't bother *me*!" the Duchess groaned. "I've never been able to stand numbers!"

With that, she went back to nursing her baby, singing a strange sort of lullaby to it and giving the child a violent shake at the end of every line:

> "Speak roughly to your little boy,  
> And smack him when he sneezes;  
> He only does it to annoy,  
> Because he knows it teases!"  
>  
> **Chorus**  
> (joined by the cook and the baby):  
>  
> "Wow! Wow! Wow!"  

While the Duchess sang the second verse, she kept tossing the baby violently up and down in the air. The poor little thing was wailing so loudly that Alice could barely make out the words:

> "I speak severely to my boy,  
> I smack him when he sneezes;  
> For he can totally enjoy  
> The pepper when he pleases!"  
>  
> **Chorus**:  
>  
> "Wow! Wow! Wow!"  

"Here! You can hold him for a bit if you want!" the Duchess shouted at Alice, tossing the baby straight at her. "I have to go get ready to play croquet with the Queen!"

With that, she rushed out of the room. The cook threw a frying pan after her as she went, but it just missed.

Alice caught the baby with no small amount of difficulty. It was an oddly shaped little creature that kept sticking its arms and legs out in every direction at once. *Just like a starfish,* Alice thought. The poor little thing was snorting like a steam engine when she caught it, constantly curling into a tight ball and then springing straight out again. For the first minute or two, it was all she could do just to keep a grip on it.

As soon as she figured out the trick to holding it—which was to fold it into a sort of knot and keep a tight grip on its right ear and left foot so it couldn't untangle itself—she carried it out into the fresh open air.

*If I don't take this child with me,* Alice thought, *they're bound to kill it in a day or two. Leaving it behind would be practically murder!*

She said those last words out loud, and the little creature gave a grunt in reply (it had finally stopped sneezing by now).

"Don't grunt," Alice said. "That's no proper way to express yourself."

The baby grunted again, and Alice looked anxiously into its face to see what was wrong. There could be no mistake about it: it had an exceptionally upturned nose, looking far more like a snout than a human nose. Its eyes were also turning remarkably small for a baby. All in all, Alice didn't like the look of it one bit.

*Maybe it was just sobbing,* she thought, and looked closely into its eyes again to see if there were any tears.

Nope, not a single tear.

"If you're going to turn into a pig, my dear," Alice said seriously, "I won't have anything more to do with you. Consider yourself warned!"

The poor little creature sobbed again—or grunted, it was impossible to tell which—and they walked along together in silence for a while.

Alice was just beginning to wonder, *Now, what in the world am I going to do with this creature when I get it home?* when it let out another grunt—so loud and violent that she looked down into its face in alarm.

This time, there could be *no* mistake about it: it was neither more nor less than a pig. Carrying it any farther would be completely absurd.

So she set the little creature down on the ground, feeling immense relief as she watched it trot peacefully away into the woods.

"If it had grown up," Alice said to herself, "it would have made a dreadfully ugly child. But it makes rather a handsome pig, I think."

She started thinking about several other children she knew who might make very fine pigs, and was just musing to herself, *If only people knew the right trick to transform them—* when she jumped in surprise. There was the Cheshire Cat, sitting on the branch of a tree just a few yards away.

The Cat merely grinned when it saw Alice. It looked good-natured, she thought, but it also had *very* long claws and an awful lot of teeth, so she felt it ought to be treated with respect.

"Cheshire Puss," she began rather timidly, not knowing at all whether it would like the name. The Cat only stretched its grin a little wider.

*Well, it seems pleased so far,* thought Alice, and she went on: "Would you please tell me which way I ought to go from here?"

"That depends a good deal on where you want to get to," said the Cat.

"I don't much care where—" said Alice.

"Then it doesn't matter which way you go," said the Cat.

"—as long as I get *somewhere*," Alice added by way of explanation.

"Oh, you're bound to do that," said the Cat, "if you only walk long enough."

Alice had to admit there was no arguing with that logic, so she tried another question: "What kind of people live around here?"

"In *that* direction," the Cat said, waving its right paw in a circle, "lives a Hatter. And in *that* direction," waving the other paw, "lives a March Hare. Visit whichever one you like—they're both completely mad."

"But I don't want to go among mad people," Alice objected.

"Oh, you can't help that," said the Cat. "We're all mad here. I'm mad. You're mad."

"How do you know I'm mad?" asked Alice.

"You must be," said the Cat, "or you wouldn't have come here."

Alice didn't think that proved anything at all, but she pressed on: "And how do you know that *you're* mad?"

"To begin with," said the Cat, "a dog isn't mad. You grant that?"

"I suppose so," said Alice.

"Well, then," the Cat went on, "you see, a dog growls when it's angry, and wags its tail when it's pleased. Now, *I* growl when I'm pleased, and wag my tail when I'm angry. Therefore, I'm mad."

"*I* call that purring, not growling," said Alice.

"Call it whatever you like," said the Cat. "Are you playing croquet with the Queen today?"

"I'd love to very much," said Alice, "but I haven't been invited yet."

"You'll see me there," said the Cat—and vanished.

Alice wasn't even especially surprised by this; she was getting so used to strange things happening. While she was still staring at the spot where it had been, it suddenly popped back into view.

"By the way, what ever became of the baby?" the Cat asked. "I almost forgot to ask."

"It turned into a pig," Alice answered calmly, speaking as casually as if the Cat had simply strolled back into the room.

"Thought it would," said the Cat, and vanished again.

Alice waited a little while, half-expecting to see it again, but it didn't appear. After a minute or two, she started walking in the direction where the March Hare was said to live.

"I've seen hatters before," she told herself. "The March Hare will be much more interesting. And since it's May, maybe it won't be raving mad—at least not as crazy as it was in March."

Just as she said this, she looked up, and there was the Cat again, perched comfortably on a tree branch.

"Did you say *pig*, or *fig*?" asked the Cat.

"I said *pig*," replied Alice. "And I really wish you wouldn't keep appearing and vanishing so suddenly! You make my head spin!"

"All right," said the Cat.

This time, it vanished very slowly, starting at the tip of its tail and ending with its grin, which lingered floating in the air for quite a while after the rest of its body was gone.

"Well!" thought Alice. "I've often seen a cat without a grin, but a grin without a cat! That is the most curious thing I ever saw in all my life!"

She hadn't gone much farther before she came in sight of the March Hare's house. She was pretty sure it was the right place, because the chimneys were shaped like rabbit ears and the roof was thatched with fur. It was such a large house that she didn't want to get any closer until she took another nibble from the left-hand piece of mushroom, growing herself up to about two feet tall. Even then, she walked up toward it rather timidly, saying to herself, "What if it really is raving mad after all? I almost wish I'd gone to see the Hatter instead!"
