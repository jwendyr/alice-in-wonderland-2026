# Chapter 4: The Rabbit Sends in a Little Bill

It was the White Rabbit, trotting slowly back again and looking around anxiously as it went, as if it had lost something. Alice heard it muttering to itself: "The Duchess! The Duchess! Oh, my dear paws! Oh, my fur and whiskers! She’ll have me executed, as sure as ferrets are ferrets! Where on earth could I have dropped them?"

Alice guessed in an instant that it was searching for the fan and the pair of white kid leather gloves. Being naturally good-natured, she began hunting around for them too, but they were nowhere to be found. Everything seemed to have changed since her swim in the pool of tears, and the grand hall with the glass table and the tiny door had vanished completely.

Before long, the Rabbit noticed Alice as she searched around, and barked at her in an angry tone, "Mary Ann! What *are* you doing out here? Run home this instant and fetch me a pair of gloves and a fan! Quick, now!"

Alice was so startled and frightened that she ran off at once in the direction it pointed, without even attempting to explain the mistake it had made.

"He thinks I’m his housemaid!" she said to herself as she ran. "How surprised he’ll be when he finds out who I really am! But I’d better take him his fan and gloves—that is, if I can find them."

Just as she said this, she came upon a neat little house. On the front door was a shiny brass plate engraved with the name "W. RABBIT." She walked in without knocking and hurried upstairs, terrified she might run into the real Mary Ann and be kicked out of the house before finding the fan and gloves.

"How strange it feels," Alice said to herself, "to be running errands for a rabbit! I suppose Dinah will be sending me on errands next!"

She began imagining how that would go:

"Miss Alice! Come here this instant and get ready for your walk!"
"Coming in a minute, Nanny! But I have to make sure the mouse doesn’t get out!"

"Only I don’t think," Alice went on, "they’d let Dinah stay in the house if she started bossing people around like that!"

By this time, she had found her way into a tidy little room with a table by the window. On it, just as she had hoped, lay a fan and two or three pairs of tiny white kid gloves. She picked up the fan and a pair of gloves and was just about to leave the room when her eyes caught a little bottle sitting near the mirror.

There was no label this time with the words "DRINK ME," but all the same, she uncorked it and brought it to her lips.

"I know *something* interesting always happens whenever I eat or drink anything down here," she told herself. "So I’ll just see what this bottle does. I really hope it makes me grow big again, because I’m thoroughly tired of being such a tiny little thing!"

It did indeed—and far faster than she had expected! Before she had even drunk half the bottle, her head was pressing against the ceiling, and she had to stoop to keep her neck from breaking.

She hastily set the bottle down, saying to herself, "That’s quite enough! I hope I won’t grow any more—as it is, I can’t even get out the door! I really wish I hadn’t drunk quite so much!"

Too late to wish that! She kept growing and growing, and very soon had to drop to her knees on the floor. Within another minute, there wasn’t even room for that, and she tried lying down with one elbow wedged against the door and her other arm curled around her head.

Still she went on growing! As a last resort, she stuck one arm out the window and thrust one foot up the chimney, telling herself, "Now I can’t do a single thing more, whatever happens. What *will* become of me?"

Luckily for Alice, the magic potion had now reached its full effect, and she grew no larger. Still, it was terribly uncomfortable, and since there seemed to be no chance whatever of ever getting out of the room again, it was no wonder she felt miserable.

"It was so much nicer at home," poor Alice thought, "when I wasn’t constantly growing bigger and smaller, and being bossed around by mice and rabbits. I almost wish I hadn’t gone down that rabbit hole—and yet... and yet... it really is rather fascinating, this kind of life! I do wonder what *could* have happened to me! Whenever I read fairy tales, I imagined that sort of thing never actually happened, and now here I am right in the middle of one! Someone ought to write a book about me—they really should! When I grow up, I’ll write one myself... but I’m already grown up now," she added sorrowfully. "At least, there’s no room to grow up any more *in here*!"

"Then again," thought Alice, "will I *never* get any older than I am right now? That would be a comfort, in a way—never having to be an old woman—but then, to always have school lessons to study! Oh, I wouldn’t like *that* at all!"

"Oh, you silly Alice!" she answered herself. "How could you do lessons in here? Why, there’s barely room for *you*, and no room at all for any textbooks!"

She went on like this, taking first one side and then the other, holding quite a lively conversation with herself. But after a few minutes, she heard a voice outside and stopped to listen.

"Mary Ann! Mary Ann!" shouted the voice. "Fetch me my gloves this instant!"

Then came the light patter of feet on the stairs. Alice knew it was the Rabbit coming to look for her, and she trembled until the whole house shook—completely forgetting that she was now about a thousand times larger than the Rabbit and had no reason at all to be afraid of it.

Soon the Rabbit came up to the door and tried to push it open. But since the door opened inward and Alice’s elbow was pressed hard against it, the attempt was a total failure.

Alice heard it say to itself, "Then I’ll go around and get in through the window."

"*That* you won’t!" thought Alice.

She waited until she imagined she heard the Rabbit right under the window, then suddenly opened her hand wide and made a quick snatch in the air. She didn’t catch anything, but she heard a little shriek, a thud, and a loud crash of shattered glass, from which she concluded it was quite likely he had tumbled into a cucumber frame or something similar.

Next came an angry voice—the Rabbit’s: "Pat! Pat! Where are you?"

Then a voice she had never heard before called back, "Sure then, I’m right here! Digging for apples, yer honor!"

"Digging for apples, my foot!" said the Rabbit furiously. "Come over here and help me out of *this*!" (Sounds of more glass crunching.)

"Now tell me, Pat, what’s that sticking out of the window?"

"Sure, it’s an arm, yer honor!" (He pronounced it "arr-um.")

"An arm, you goose! Who ever saw one that size? Why, it fills the whole window!"

"Sure it does, yer honor—but it’s an arm all the same!"

"Well, it has no business being there, at any rate! Go and take it away!"

A long silence followed, and Alice could only catch occasional whispers, such as:

"Sure, I don’t like the look of it, yer honor, not at all, at all!"
"Do as I tell you, you coward!"

Finally, Alice opened her hand again and made another snatch through the air. This time there were *two* little shrieks, followed by more crashing glass.

"What an awful lot of cucumber frames there must be!" thought Alice. "I wonder what they’ll try next! As for pulling me out through the window, I only wish they *could*! I certainly don’t want to stay stuck in here any longer!"

She waited for a good while without hearing anything more. At last came the rumble of little cartwheels and the sound of a large crowd of voices all talking at once. She could make out the words:

"Where’s the other ladder?"
"Why, I was only supposed to bring one! Bill’s got the other—"
"Bill! Bring it here, boy!"
"Here, set them up at this corner—"
"No, tie them together first—they don’t reach even halfway up yet!"
"Oh, they’ll do just fine; don’t be so picky!"
"Here, Bill! Grab hold of this rope—"
"Will the roof hold?"
"Watch that loose slate—look out, it’s slipping! Heads up below!" (A loud crash echoed outside.)
"Now, who did that?"
"It was Bill, I bet!"
"Who’s going down the chimney?"
"No way, not me! *You* do it!"
"I certainly won’t!"
"Bill’s going down! Here, Bill! The master says you’re to go down the chimney!"

"Oh! So Bill has to come down the chimney, does he?" Alice said to herself. "Why, they seem to dump everything onto Bill! I wouldn’t want to be in Bill’s shoes for anything. This fireplace is narrow, to be sure, but I *think* I can manage a kick!"

She reached her foot as far down into the chimney flue as she could and waited until she heard a little animal (she couldn’t tell what kind it was) scratching and scrambling around in the chimney just above her. Then, whispering to herself, "This is Bill," she gave one sharp, solid kick and waited to see what would happen next.

The first thing she heard was a general shout of, "There goes Bill!"

Then the Rabbit’s voice yelled, "Catch him, you by the hedge!"

Then came a moment of silence, followed by a flurry of confused voices:

"Hold up his head!"
"Give him some brandy!"
"Don’t choke him!"
"How was it, old pal? What happened to you? Tell us all about it!"

Finally came a feeble, squeaking little voice ("That’s Bill," thought Alice):

"Well, I hardly know... No more, thank you; I’m better now... but I’m far too shaken up to tell you! All I know is, something came at me like a jack-in-the-box, and up I went like a rocket!"

"You sure did, old pal!" the others agreed.

"We must burn the house down!" said the Rabbit’s voice.

At that, Alice yelled out as loud as she could, "If you do, I’ll set Dinah on you!"

An instant, dead silence fell over everything. Alice thought to herself, "I wonder what they *will* do next! If they had any sense at all, they’d take the roof off."

After a minute or two, they began moving around again, and Alice heard the Rabbit say, "A wheelbarrow full will do, to begin with."

"A wheelbarrow full of *what*?" Alice thought.

She didn’t have to wonder for long, because the next instant a shower of little pebbles came rattling through the window, and several of them hit her right in the face.

"I’ll put a stop to this!" she told herself, and shouted, "You’d better not do that again!"

Which produced another dead silence.

Alice noticed with surprise that the pebbles lying on the floor were all turning into little cakes, and a brilliant idea popped into her head.

"If I eat one of these cakes," she thought, "it’s bound to make *some* change in my size. And since it can’t possibly make me any larger, I suppose it must make me smaller!"

So she swallowed one of the cakes and was delighted to find that she began shrinking right away. As soon as she was small enough to get through the door, she dashed out of the house and found quite a crowd of small animals and birds waiting outside.

The poor little lizard, Bill, was in the middle, propped up by two guinea pigs who were feeding him something from a bottle.

They all rushed toward Alice the moment she appeared, but she ran off as fast as her legs could carry her and soon found herself safe in a dense wood.

"The first thing I have to do," Alice said to herself as she wandered through the woods, "is grow back to my proper size. And the second thing is to find my way into that lovely garden. I think that’s the best plan."

It sounded like an excellent plan, no doubt—very neat and simply arranged. The only trouble was that she didn’t have the slightest idea how to carry it out. And while she was peering anxiously among the trees, a sharp little bark right over her head made her look up in a hurry.

An enormous puppy was looking down at her with huge, round eyes, awkwardly stretching out one paw to touch her.

"Poor little thing!" Alice said coaxingly, trying hard to whistle to it. But all the while she was terribly frightened that it might be hungry, in which case it would probably gobble her right up, no matter how sweetly she spoke.

Hardly knowing what she was doing, she picked up a little stick and held it out toward the puppy.

Instantly, the puppy sprang into the air with all four paws off the ground, gave a yelp of pure delight, and charged at the stick, pretending to shake and chew it. Alice ducked behind a tall thistle to keep from being trampled. The moment she popped out on the other side, the puppy made another rush at the stick and tumbled head over heels in its eagerness to grab it.

Alice, thinking this was very much like playing a game of catch with a giant carthorse and expecting to get crushed under its paws at any second, darted around the thistle again. Then the puppy started a series of playful fake charges at the stick—darting forward a few inches, bounding backward several paces, and barking hoarsely the whole time—until at last it plopped down a little ways away, panting with its tongue lolling out of its mouth and its big eyes half shut.

This seemed like the perfect chance to escape. Alice took off at once, running until she was completely exhausted and out of breath, and until the puppy’s barks were only a faint sound in the distance.

"Still, what an adorable little puppy that was!" Alice said, leaning against a buttercup to catch her breath and fanning herself with one of its leaves. "I would have loved to teach it tricks—if only I’d been the right size to do it! Oh dear, I almost forgot that I need to grow back up again! Let’s see... how is that to be done? I suppose I ought to eat or drink something, but the big question is: what?"

The big question certainly was: what?

Alice looked all around at the flowers and the giant blades of grass, but she didn’t see anything that looked suitable to eat or drink under the circumstances.

There was a large mushroom growing nearby, roughly the same height as herself. After looking under it, around both sides of it, and behind it, it occurred to her that she might as well check to see what was on top.

She stretched up on tiptoe and peeked over the rim of the mushroom cap. Her eyes immediately met those of a large blue caterpillar, who was sitting on top with its arms folded, quietly smoking a long hookah pipe and paying not the slightest bit of attention to her or anyone else.
